\section{Visual Representation}
\label{sec:visual-representation}

In order to segment objects across videos we need to segment objects in individual frames (i.e. images), and in order to segment objects in images, we need to represent them numerically. Mathematically speaking, we aim at representing an image with a vector of fixed length called \textit{embedding}. How pixels
become such vectors, and what those vectors come to encode, is therefore the
natural starting point for the theory that follows. 

This section builds that representation from the ground up, beginning with the sequence model that
underpins all modern visual backbones, extending it to images, and finally
showing how the most informative of these representations can be learned without
any human input in the form of labels. We define the notation with care because it recurs in every subsequent section.

\subsection{The Transformer}
\label{subsec:transformer}

In the early days of Machine Learning, sequence models relied on recurrence, which processes tokens one at a
time, but struggles to relate positions that are far apart. Vaswani et al.\ in 2017
\cite{vaswani2017attention} introduced a novel paradigm with the
\textit{Transformer}, an architecture built almost exclusively around the attention
mechanism and designed originally for machine translation. At its core, attention lets every element of a sequence look at every
other element and aggregate information from those it deems relevant, in a single
parallel operation. We ground the explanation that follows in one running example, the
translation of the sentence ``the animal did not cross the street because it was too
tired'', where producing a correct translation requires linking the pronoun ``it'' to
``animal'' several words earlier.

We represent the input as a sequence of $n$ tokens stacked into a matrix
$\mm{X}\in\R^{n\times\dmodel}$, where $\dmodel$ is the embedding dimension shared
throughout the model. From $\mm{X}$ the model derives three projections through
learned weight matrices, the queries $\query=\mm{X}\mm{W}_Q$, the keys
$\key=\mm{X}\mm{W}_K$, and the values $\val=\mm{X}\mm{W}_V$. Intuitively, each token
emits a query for describing what it is looking for,  a key for describing what it
offers, and a value for holding the content it would pass on if attended.
Scaled dot-product attention then compares queries against keys and uses the result
to take a weighted average of the values,

\begin{equation}
  \attn(\query,\key,\val)
  = \softmax\!\left(\frac{\query\key\transpose}{\sqrt{\dkey}}\right)\val .
  \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}

Here, the product $\query\key\transpose$ scores every query against every key, so
that entry $(i,j)$ measures how strongly token $i$ should attend to token $j$. The
scaling factor $1/\sqrt{\dkey}$, where $\dkey$ is the dimension of each key, prevents the scores from 
growing with dimension, which avoids the softmax going into regions of
small gradient and therefore enhances training. The row-wise $\softmax$ turns each row of scores into a
probability distribution over the sequence, and multiplying by $\val$ replaces each
token with the convex combination of values its distribution selects. The output is
again an $n\times\dmodel$ sequence in which every token has been rewritten as a
mixture of the content it found relevant. In our example, the distribution for the token
``it'' concentrates its mass on ``animal'', so the updated representation of the pronoun
absorbs the meaning of the noun it refers to.

A single attention operation captures only one notion of relevance and relationship among inputs. To let the model attend to several relationships at once, the Transformer runs $h$ attention operations in
parallel, each with its own projections and each operating on a slice of dimension
$\dhead=\dmodel/h$. Returning to the example, one head can resolve the reference of
``it'' while another tracks which verb governs each noun, so that a single layer
represents several such relationships together. The resulting heads are concatenated and linearly mixed,

\begin{equation}
  \mhattn(\mm{X})
  = \big[\,\mathrm{head}_1;\ \dots;\ \mathrm{head}_h\,\big]\,\mm{W}_O,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{head}_i=\attn(\query_i,\key_i,\val_i),
  \label{eq:mha}
\end{equation}

where each $\mathrm{head}_i$ applies \cref{eq:attention} with head-specific
projections and $\mm{W}_O$ recombines them into the model dimension. Because
attention is permutation invariant, the architecture has no inherent sense of order;
position is injected by adding a positional encoding to the input embeddings, either
the fixed sinusoidal functions of the original work or a set of learned vectors. Without
it the example sentence would carry no notion of word order, and ``it'' could no longer be
tied to the noun that precedes it. Each
attention block is wrapped in a residual connection and layer normalisation
\cite{vaswani2017attention} and followed by a position-wise feed-forward network,
and these blocks are stacked into deep encoders, with the full architecture illustrated in \cref{fig:transformer-arch}. Two arrangements are common, namely
an encoder-decoder stack for sequence transduction, as in the original translation
model, and an encoder-only stack for
producing contextual representations, the latter being the one relevant to vision.
The expressive power of attention carries a cost. Because
\cref{eq:attention} compares every token with every other, both computation and
memory grow as $O(n^2)$ in the sequence length. This scaling limit shaped the design choices 
of the vision models built on top of it and motivated several future works aimed at optimization 
while retaining output quality.

\subsection{The Vision Transformer}
\label{subsec:vit}

Attention was conceived for words, yet nothing in \cref{eq:attention} assumes the
tokens are linguistic. Dosovitskiy et al.\ in 2021 \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} made
this explicit with the \textit{Vision Transformer (ViT)}, which applies a standard
Transformer encoder to images by first turning a picture into a sequence, as illustrated in \cref{fig:vit-arch}. The image
$\img$ is partitioned into a grid of $h\times w$ square patches that do not overlap,
typically of side sixteen pixels, and each patch is flattened and linearly projected
into a token of dimension $\dmodel$. Letting $\patch_i$ denote the $i$-th patch token and
$\mm{E}$ the shared projection, the encoder receives

\begin{equation}
  \mm{Z}_0
  = \big[\,\cls;\ \mm{E}\patch_1;\ \mm{E}\patch_2;\ \dots;\ \mm{E}\patch_{\npatch}\,\big]
    + \mm{E}_{\text{pos}},
  \label{eq:patch-embed}
\end{equation}

a sequence of $\npatch=h\,w$ patch tokens to which two further elements are added.
The first is a learnable classification token $\cls$, prepended to the sequence and
referring to no pixels of its own. Instead, through the attention layers, it aggregates information
from every patch and serves as a single global summary of the image. The second is
the matrix of learned positional embeddings $\mm{E}_{\text{pos}}$, which restores the
spatial arrangement that flattening discarded. From this point the model is an
ordinary Transformer encoder, with the patch tokens it returns as descriptors of parts of the image and 
the final state of $\cls$ is an image-level descriptor. The number
of patches $\npatch$ grows quadratically with image resolution.

This flexibility comes at the price of data. Lacking the spatial inductive biases
that convolutional networks build in by design, the Vision Transformer can learn
locality and translation structure only from seeing examples. Its authors report
competitive results only after supervised pre-training on very large labelled datasets such
as \textit{ImageNet-21k} and the proprietary \textit{JFT-300M}. The same year, Touvron et al. proved 
that this scale is not strictly necessary with \textit{DeiT} \cite{touvron2021deit}. They trained competitive Vision Transformers on \textit{ImageNet} alone by combining strong augmentation with a distillation token
that learns from a convolutional teacher. While the transformer's application is general, 
its tendency to supervision and scaling inefficiency raise questions about its applicability in computer vision.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Visual Features}
\label{subsec:dino}

Supervised pre-training scales only as far as annotation budgets allow, and human
labels are expensive, narrow on describing what an image contains, and possibly biased. Self-supervised
learning solves these constraints by replacing the supervised label with a pretext task
defined on the data itself, whose solution forces the model to learn general representations. Several families of pretext task exist, among them predicting an artificial
transformation of the input, contrasting different views of the same image, and
reconstructing deliberately corrupted inputs. We focus on the self-distillation
approach because it produces the features our pipeline relies on.

\textit{DINO}, introduced by Caron et al.\ in 2021 \cite{dinov1}, frames
self-supervision as distillation with no labels, with the overall scheme shown in \cref{fig:dino-architecture}. It defines two networks with identical
architecture, a student $g_{\theta_s}$ and a teacher $g_{\theta_t}$, each mapping an image to a distribution over $K$ dimensions, set to 4096 in the original paper. 
While these $K$ dimensions are undefined so the model can establish their abstract meaning during training, conceptually they can be though of as classes describing to which an image can belong to.
The two networks see different augmented crops of the same image, like small local crops given only to the student and larger global crops given to the teacher, and the
student is trained so that its prediction matches the teacher's across these views.
The teacher's distribution is formed by a softmax with two deliberate distortions,

\begin{equation}
  P_t(\img)^{(k)}
  = \frac{\exp\!\big((g_{\theta_t}(\img)^{(k)}-c^{(k)})/\kappa_t\big)}
         {\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\!\big((g_{\theta_t}(\img)^{(j)}-c^{(j)})/\kappa_t\big)},
  \label{eq:dino-teacher}
\end{equation}

and the student is optimised to minimise the cross-entropy $-\sum_k P_t(\img)^{(k)}\log P_s(\img)^{(k)}$ against it.\footnote{The original work uses $\tau$, but we reserve it to represent the video frame index.} Here, the authors propose two techniques to address representational collapse, the central danger of training without labels, where the network maps
every image to the same output and the loss is trivially minimised. The vector $c$
represents a running centre, obtained by removing the running mean. This prevents any single prototype dimension from dominating and absorbing all images. The parameter $\kappa_t$ dictates the sharpness of the teacher distribution, keeping it from drifting to a uniform shape. The authors note that each guards against a
different collapse mode, and the methods keep training stable only when applied jointly.

The teacher is never trained by gradient descent. Its weights are instead an
exponential moving average of the student's,

\begin{equation}
  \theta_t \leftarrow \lambda\,\theta_t + (1-\lambda)\,\theta_s,
  \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}

with momentum $\lambda$ close to one, so that the teacher is a slowly evolving
ensemble of recent students. This lag makes the teacher a more stable target than the student itself and is what allows the distillation loop to bootstrap a representation from random initialisation. 

This training procedure gives rise to a striking property. The attention maps of the $\cls$ token attend to coherent object regions, and the patch tokens carry enough semantic structure that scene layout and object boundaries can be read off them directly, despite no segmentation label ever entering training, as shown in \cref{fig:dino-attention}.

\textit{DINOv2}, presented by Oquab et al.\ in 2023 \cite{oquab2023dinov2}, elevates this
architecture into a general-purpose feature extractor. Rather than scaling data
indiscriminately, the authors assemble a large curated corpus, \textit{LVD-142M}, and train larger Vision
Transformers on it with a combined image-level and patch-level objective. They further
observe that the patch features of a plain ViT are corrupted by a small number of
high-norm outlier tokens, and remove them by appending a handful of dedicated ``register
tokens'' to the sequence whose sole role is to absorb this global information and leave the patch
features clean. The resulting features are strong enough to be used directly in downstream classification or prediction tasks, reaching competitive accuracy under a linear probe or even a nearest-neighbour classifier, and they remain the visual backbone against which our cross-view experiments are measured.
The most recent iteration, \textit{DINOv3} \cite{dinov3}, extends this line further
still and confirms its trajectory.

These representations are useful because they support principled mathematical comparisons of abstract information like the meaning of images. The final $\cls$ state
provides a global descriptor $\feat$ of an image, and once two such descriptors are
$L_2$-normalised their dot product $\cossim{\feat_a}{\feat_b}$ is exactly their cosine
similarity, acting as a bounded score of semantic agreement which enables retrieval and
re-ranking. The same comparison applies at the patch level, where matching patch
descriptors across two images yields correspondences between their regions, a valuable property
for cross-view object correspondence to which we return in \cref{sec:correspondence-segmentation}.

Another pretext task that is relevant for our pipeline is the \textit{Masked Autoencoder (MAE)} \cite{he2022mae}. This method masks a large fraction of an image's patches, around three quarters, and trains a Vision Transformer
encoder together with a lightweight decoder to reconstruct the missing pixels from the
visible ones. The high masking ratio makes reconstruction non-trivial and forces the
encoder to learn the structure of the scene rather than pixel-level details. MAE is used to train the image encoder of the Segment Anything Model \cite{kirillov2023sam}, which we will describe in the next section.

The elements of our pipeline build on the representations defined here.  DINOv2 defines the latent space in which O-MaMa selects candidate masks using patch features, a mechanism we develop in \cref{sec:correspondence-segmentation}. The cosine retrieval scheme on $\cls$ descriptors powers a frame-diversity selection strategy that we retain as a baseline in our experiments. The same Vision Transformer encoder reappears as the image branch of the language-grounded models in \cref{sec:language-grounding}, within the multimodal backbone of \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}, and in the video segmenters of
\cref{sec:addition-of-time}.
