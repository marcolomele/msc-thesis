\section{Visual Representation}
\label{sec:visual-representation}

Every component of our pipeline operates on the same primitive object, namely a
fixed-length vector that encodes what a region of an image contains. How pixels
become such vectors, and what those vectors come to encode, is therefore the
natural starting point for the theory that follows. This section builds that
representation from the ground up, beginning with the sequence model that
underpins all modern visual backbones, extending it to images, and finally
showing how the most informative of these representations can be learned without
any human labels. The notation introduced here, both the attention machinery and
the patch and token symbols, recurs in every subsequent section, so we define it
with care.

\subsection{The Transformer}
\label{subsec:transformer}

Sequence models were long dominated by recurrence, which processes tokens one at a
time and struggles to relate positions that are far apart. Vaswani et al.\ in 2017
\cite{vaswani2017attention} dispensed with recurrence entirely and proposed the
\textit{Transformer}, an architecture built almost exclusively around the attention
mechanism. At its core, attention lets every element of a sequence look at every
other element and aggregate information from those it deems relevant, in a single
parallel operation.

We represent the input as a sequence of $n$ tokens stacked into a matrix
$\mm{X}\in\R^{n\times\dmodel}$, where $\dmodel$ is the embedding dimension shared
throughout the model. From $\mm{X}$ the model derives three projections through
learned weight matrices, the queries $\query=\mm{X}\mm{W}_Q$, the keys
$\key=\mm{X}\mm{W}_K$, and the values $\val=\mm{X}\mm{W}_V$. Intuitively, each token
emits a query describing what it is looking for, advertises a key describing what it
offers, and carries a value holding the content it would pass on if attended to.
Scaled dot-product attention then compares queries against keys and uses the result
to take a weighted average of the values,

\begin{equation}
  \attn(\query,\key,\val)
  = \softmax\!\left(\frac{\query\key\transpose}{\sqrt{\dkey}}\right)\val .
  \label{eq:attention}
\end{equation}

Here, the product $\query\key\transpose$ scores every query against every key, so
that entry $(i,j)$ measures how strongly token $i$ should attend to token $j$. The
scaling factor $1/\sqrt{\dkey}$, where $\dkey$ is the dimension of each key, keeps
these scores from growing with dimension and pushing the softmax into regions of
vanishing gradient. The row-wise $\softmax$ turns each row of scores into a
probability distribution over the sequence, and multiplying by $\val$ replaces each
token with the convex combination of values its distribution selects. The output is
again an $n\times\dmodel$ sequence in which every token has been rewritten as a
mixture of the content it found relevant.

A single such operation captures one notion of relevance. To let the model attend to
several relationships at once, the Transformer runs $h$ attention operations in
parallel, each with its own projections and each operating on a slice of dimension
$\dhead=\dmodel/h$. The resulting heads are concatenated and linearly mixed,

\begin{equation}
  \mhattn(\mm{X})
  = \big[\,\mathrm{head}_1;\ \dots;\ \mathrm{head}_h\,\big]\,\mm{W}_O,
  \qquad
  \mathrm{head}_i=\attn(\query_i,\key_i,\val_i),
  \label{eq:mha}
\end{equation}

where each $\mathrm{head}_i$ applies \cref{eq:attention} with head-specific
projections and $\mm{W}_O$ recombines them into the model dimension. Because
attention is permutation invariant, the architecture has no inherent sense of order;
position is injected by adding a positional encoding to the input embeddings, either
the fixed sinusoidal functions of the original work or a set of learned vectors. Each
attention block is wrapped in a residual connection and layer normalisation
\cite{vaswani2017attention} and followed by a position-wise feed-forward network,
and these blocks are stacked into deep encoders. Two arrangements are common, namely
an encoder-decoder stack for sequence transduction and an encoder-only stack for
producing contextual representations, the latter being the one relevant to vision.
The expressive power of attention carries a cost. Because
\cref{eq:attention} compares every token with every other, both computation and
memory grow as $O(n^2)$ in the sequence length, a scaling that will repeatedly
shape the design choices of the vision models built on top of it.

\subsection{The Vision Transformer}
\label{subsec:vit}

Attention was conceived for words, yet nothing in \cref{eq:attention} assumes the
tokens are linguistic. Dosovitskiy et al.\ in 2021 \cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} made
this explicit with the \textit{Vision Transformer (ViT)}, which applies a standard
Transformer encoder to images by first turning a picture into a sequence. The image
$\img$ is partitioned into a grid of $h\times w$ square patches that do not overlap,
typically of side sixteen pixels, and each patch is flattened and linearly projected
into a token of dimension $\dmodel$. Writing $\patch_i$ for the $i$-th patch token and
$\mm{E}$ for the shared projection, the encoder receives

\begin{equation}
  \mm{Z}_0
  = \big[\,\cls;\ \mm{E}\patch_1;\ \mm{E}\patch_2;\ \dots;\ \mm{E}\patch_{\npatch}\,\big]
    + \mm{E}_{\text{pos}},
  \label{eq:patch-embed}
\end{equation}

a sequence of $\npatch=h\,w$ patch tokens to which two further elements are added.
The first is a learnable classification token $\cls$, prepended to the sequence and
owning no pixels of its own; through the attention layers it aggregates information
from every patch and serves as a single global summary of the image. The second is
the matrix of learned positional embeddings $\mm{E}_{\text{pos}}$, which restores the
spatial arrangement that flattening discarded. From this point the model is an
ordinary Transformer encoder, and the patch tokens it returns are spatially localised
descriptors while the final state of $\cls$ is an image-level descriptor. The number
of patches $\npatch$ grows quadratically with image resolution, so the $O(n^2)$ cost
of attention ties resolution directly to compute and motivates much later work on
efficient variants.

This flexibility comes at the price of data. Lacking the spatial inductive biases
that convolutional networks build in by construction, the Vision Transformer learns
locality and translation structure only from examples, and Dosovitskiy et al.\ found
it competitive only after supervised pre-training on very large labelled corpora such
as \textit{ImageNet-21k} and the proprietary \textit{JFT-300M}. That this scale is
not strictly necessary was shown soon after by Touvron et al.\ in 2021 with
\textit{DeiT} \cite{touvron2021deit}, who trained competitive Vision Transformers on
\textit{ImageNet} alone by combining strong augmentation with a distillation token
that learns from a convolutional teacher. The architecture is therefore general, but
its appetite for supervision raised the question that the next section answers,
namely whether the labels can be removed altogether.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Visual Features}
\label{subsec:dino}

Supervised pre-training scales only as far as annotation budgets allow, and human
labels are both expensive and a narrow window onto what an image contains. Self-supervised
learning sidesteps this constraint by replacing the human label with a pretext task
defined on the data itself, whose solution forces the network to learn useful
structure. Several families of pretext task exist, among them predicting an artificial
transformation of the input, contrasting different views of the same image, and
reconstructing deliberately corrupted inputs; we focus on the self-distillation
approach that produces the features our pipeline relies on and treat the others only
where they reappear later.

\textit{DINO}, introduced by Caron et al.\ in 2021 \cite{dinov1}, frames
self-supervision as distillation with no labels. Two networks of identical
architecture are maintained, a student $g_{\theta_s}$ and a teacher $g_{\theta_t}$,
each mapping an image to a distribution over $K$ abstract prototype dimensions. The
two networks see different augmented crops of the same image, including small local
crops given only to the student and larger global crops given to the teacher, and the
student is trained so that its prediction matches the teacher's across these views.
The teacher's distribution is formed by a softmax with two deliberate distortions,

\begin{equation}
  P_t(\img)^{(k)}
  = \frac{\exp\!\big((g_{\theta_t}(\img)^{(k)}-c^{(k)})/\kappa_t\big)}
         {\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\!\big((g_{\theta_t}(\img)^{(j)}-c^{(j)})/\kappa_t\big)},
  \label{eq:dino-teacher}
\end{equation}

and the student is optimised to minimise the cross-entropy
$-\sum_k P_t(\img)^{(k)}\log P_s(\img)^{(k)}$ against it.\footnote{We write the teacher
temperature as $\kappa_t$ where the original work uses $\tau$, reserving $\tau$ for the
video frame index per the notation contract of this chapter.} Here, the vector $c$ is
a running centre subtracted from the teacher logits, and $\kappa_t$ is a temperature
that sharpens the teacher distribution. The two together resolve the central danger of
training without labels, which is representational collapse, where the network maps
every image to the same output and the loss is trivially minimised. Centering, by
removing the running mean, prevents any single prototype dimension from dominating and
absorbing all images; sharpening, through the low temperature $\kappa_t$, keeps the
teacher distribution from drifting to the uniform one. Because each guards against a
different collapse mode, neither alone suffices, but applied jointly they keep training
stable.

The teacher is never trained by gradient descent. Its weights are instead an
exponential moving average of the student's,

\begin{equation}
  \theta_t \leftarrow \lambda\,\theta_t + (1-\lambda)\,\theta_s,
  \label{eq:ema}
\end{equation}

with momentum $\lambda$ close to one, so that the teacher is a slowly evolving
ensemble of recent students.\footnote{This is the momentum-encoder update of DINO; the
centre $c$ is maintained by an analogous moving average over each batch.} This lag
makes the teacher a more stable target than the student itself and is what allows the
distillation loop to bootstrap a representation from random initialisation. The
property that made DINO notable emerges from this procedure without ever being asked
for. The attention maps of the $\cls$ token attend to coherent object regions, and the
patch tokens carry enough semantic structure that scene layout and object boundaries can
be read off them directly, despite no segmentation label ever entering training.

\textit{DINOv2}, presented by Oquab et al.\ in 2023 \cite{oquab2023dinov2}, scales this
recipe into a general-purpose feature extractor. Rather than scale data
indiscriminately, the authors assemble a large curated corpus, \textit{LVD-142M}, by
retrieving images similar to a set of curated sources, and train larger Vision
Transformers on it with a combined image-level and patch-level objective. They further
observe that the patch features of a plain ViT are marred by a small number of
high-norm outlier tokens, and remove them by appending a handful of dedicated register
tokens to the sequence that absorb this global information and leave the patch
features clean.\footnote{The original work calls these \emph{register tokens}; they
carry no positional meaning and are discarded after the encoder.} The resulting
features are strong enough to be used directly, without adaptation, reaching
competitive accuracy under a linear probe or even a nearest-neighbour classifier, and
they remain the visual backbone against which our cross-view experiments are measured.
The most recent iteration, \textit{DINOv3} \cite{dinov3}, extends this line further
still and confirms its trajectory.

These representations are useful because they support comparison. The final $\cls$ state
provides a global descriptor $\feat$ of an image, and once two such descriptors are
$L_2$-normalised their dot product $\cossim{\feat_a}{\feat_b}$ is exactly their cosine
similarity, a bounded score of semantic agreement that underlies retrieval and
re-ranking. The same comparison applies at the patch level, where matching patch
descriptors across two images yields correspondences between their regions, a property
we return to in \cref{sec:correspondence-segmentation}.

For completeness we note one further pretext task that reappears downstream. The
\textit{Masked Autoencoder (MAE)} of He et al.\ in 2022 \cite{he2022mae} masks a large
fraction of an image's patches, around three quarters, and trains a Vision Transformer
encoder together with a lightweight decoder to reconstruct the missing pixels from the
visible ones. The high masking ratio makes reconstruction non-trivial and forces the
encoder to model the structure of the scene rather than interpolate texture. We do not
develop MAE further here, but flag it now because the image encoder of the Segment
Anything Model, central to \cref{sec:correspondence-segmentation}, is a
Vision Transformer pre-trained in exactly this fashion.

The representations defined in this section are what the rest of the pipeline composes.
DINOv2 supplies the latent space in which O-MaMa selects candidate masks across views,
and its patch features give label-free semantic correspondence between viewpoints, a
mechanism we develop in \cref{sec:correspondence-segmentation}. The cosine retrieval
scheme on $\cls$ descriptors underpins a frame-diversity selection strategy that we
retain as the \texttt{MaxInfo} ablation baseline in our experiments. The same Vision
Transformer encoder reappears throughout what follows, as the image branch of the
language-grounded models in \cref{sec:language-grounding}, within the multimodal
backbone of \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}, and in the video segmenters of
\cref{sec:addition-of-time}.
