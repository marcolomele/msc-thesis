\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis set out to test a single hypothesis, namely that natural language can serve as a bridge between frozen foundation models, and it did so on one of the most demanding settings available, cross-view object correspondence on \textit{Ego-Exo4D}. The ``two eyes and one mouth'' pipeline of \cref{chap:pipeline} carries an object across the egocentric and exocentric viewpoints through a single human-readable description, with no component trained on the benchmark and every intermediate decision open to inspection. Against the two limitations of prior work named in \cref{chap:intro}, dependence on training and the opacity of learned correspondences, this construction answers both at once. On the official test split it reaches within a few IoU points of O-MaMa, the 2025 challenge winner, and its working mode matches or exceeds even LM-EEC, the strongest published method, while remaining the only entry in \cref{tab:main} that is at once free of training, agnostic to the camera, and agnostic to the dataset.

The mechanistic analysis of \cref{chap:experiments} does not contradict that hypothesis but instead sharpens it. The deficit to the state of the art is not a uniform blur of imprecision; it is a discrete ``dead mass'' that accounts for roughly 31 to 32 percent of cases, and removing it alone would lift the average by about 0.18 IoU into LM-EEC territory. The pipeline does not degrade gracefully across the board, but rather it succeeds cleanly on most of the data and fails outright on a locatable minority. Crucially, the legibility that the language bridge provides is what turned this gap from a verdict into a diagnosis, since a pipeline of frozen and inspectable stages exposes exactly where it breaks, a property no competitor that learns end to end offers. The failures therefore mark where nuance is still missing, not where the paradigm broke down.

Reading the failure waterfall back through the pipeline turns that diagnosis into three sharp directions for future work. First, the language bridge of \cref{subsec:Stage2} should be constrained to emit a canonical noun phrase while preserving the surrounding scene context the name depends on, because naming is 59 to 81 percent of the dead mass and the failure is perceptual rather than lexical, a vision-language model nominating the larger neighbour it can actually see when the target shrinks to a fraction of a percent of the frame. The obvious remedy of zooming in backfires, since a tight crop collapses IoU from 0.411 to 0.180 by destroying the very context that identifies a small object, so the constraint must add discipline to the name without removing the scene around it. Then, an external verification gate is needed to catch the confident mis-groundings that dominate the dead mass, because these failures are silent, a mask being produced in 93 percent of them, and the pipeline's own confidence overlaps almost entirely between hits and misses. Finally, a trigger that reacquires the object on runs of empty masks would recover the propagation drift that only surfaces once the target is widened below 0.3 IoU; unlike the dead mass, this failure announces itself through a long stretch of empty predictions and is therefore exploitable. As a subordinate hedge, dense geometric matching deserves a second look as a complementary anchor signal, since RoMa was set aside only because its correspondences collapse under the ego-exo viewpoint gap \cite{roma}, and a matcher robust to extreme viewpoint change could reinforce the language signal at anchor selection without reintroducing any training (\cref{subsec:matching}).

These directions all refine a paradigm that already works rather than rescue one that failed. The broader claim the abstract opened therefore holds, that natural language can act as a reliable bridge between machine learning architectures, compressing what a learned latent space would otherwise carry into a phrase a human can read, question, and correct. The beauty of this paradigm lies in how it reconciles two goals that learned correspondence has long traded off against each other, reaching the performance of specialised architectures while keeping every step of its reasoning legible. As foundation models continue to absorb a more general understanding of the visual and linguistic world, the most capable systems may increasingly be the ones we compose rather than the ones we train, and language may remain the simplest and most human medium through which to compose them.
