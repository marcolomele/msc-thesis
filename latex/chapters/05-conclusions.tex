\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis set out to test a single hypothesis: can natural language serve as a bridge between frozen foundation models? To answer it, we addressed one of the most demanding settings in computer vision, object correspondence across the egocentric and exocentric views of \textit{Ego-Exo4D}. The ``Two Eyes and One Mouth'' pipeline of \cref{chap:pipeline} treats language as that bridge, mapping an object between two viewpoints through a single textual description, with no component trained on the benchmark. This design answers the two limitations of prior work. The first is the dependence on training, which ties the model to the distribution of the data and to the camera configuration. The second is the difficulty of explaining a correspondence, because all related works encode their reasoning in complex fusion modules and abstract learned features. On the official test split, the pipeline reaches within a few IoU points of O-MaMa, the 2025 challenge winner, and overtakes ObjectRelator. It remains the only entry in \cref{tab:main} that is at once free of training, agnostic to the camera, and agnostic to the dataset. On the validation analysis, once its failing cases are set aside, its working mode draws level with even LM-EEC, the strongest published method to date.

\paragraph{Main findings.} The mechanistic analysis of the results sharpens our initial hypothesis. The deficit to the state of the art is not random imprecision, but rather a well-identified ``dead mass'' that holds roughly a third of cases, which point to optimisations rather than unexplainable failures. The pipeline executes the cross-view correspondence on a few frames and then propagates the mask with a video tracker. When the bridge fails on those frames, nothing downstream can recover or even check the prediction, so the entire instance is lost. The pattern is clear once we break the results down by the quality of the anchor. The pipeline competes with LM-EEC wherever the anchor is good, and cedes almost every case wherever it is poor, which means that the loss is born at the bridge. Two factors push a case into the dead mass, the object's size and the name the VLM writes for it, and the naming is the more damaging of the two. Crucially, the legibility of the language bridge is what turned this gap from a verdict into a diagnosis, indicating where nuance is missing rather than where the paradigm broke down.

Following the diagnosis, we tested a set of targeted interventions. The most obvious repair was to condition the description on a vocabulary of objects plausible for the scene. However, the gain did not survive a larger validation set, which suggests that the issue lies in perception rather than in the naming of the object. We then tightened the grounding by passing the detector's box to the segmenter as a crop. This rescued the cases the pipeline was already failing, yet it corrupted the many it already handled, implying that only a hint conditional on the anchor's properties could help. We also ruled out issues with data quality, since raising the brightness of the dark egocentric frames did not help. None of these interventions closed the gap on its own. Each one further narrowed where the true fix must act, and that is the dividend of a pipeline open to inspection at every stage (\cref{sec:interventions}).

\paragraph{First tier future work.} We organize future developments in two tiers. The first concerns refinements to the pipeline to address the dead cases. First, the language bridge of \cref{subsec:Stage2} should be further studied empirically, for instance with a principled prompt optimisation campaign constraining the VLM to emit better phrases for SAM~3 Agent. Additionally, since interventions on what the VLM receives proved ineffective, the more promising lever is how the model attends to the target before it names it, given that naming dominates the dead mass and the failure is perceptual. A foveated tokenisation that concentrates patches on the candidate region, in the manner introduced by Schmidt et al. \cite{schmidt2025segmentthingfoveatedtokenization}, would impose a structural bias toward the object and let us study how VLMs translate vision into language.Second, a stronger grounding model would address the slice of the waterfall where the detector boxes a correctly named object off target. LocateAnything \cite{wang2026locateanythingfasthighqualityvisionlanguage}, presented in a 2026 technical report from NVIDIA and not yet peer reviewed, would be a natural candidate for this stage. Third, an external verification gate is needed to catch the confident mis-groundings that make up the rest of the dead mass. These failures are silent, because the pipeline almost always produces a mask, and its own confidence scores are unreliable for distinguishing between successes and misses. The lesson of the crop hint applies directly here, namely that an intervention must fire only on the failing population. Finally, additional performance gains could be made with more nuanced propagation mechanisms aimed at reacquiring the object after a run of empty masks produced despite a good anchor. This could be achieved with heuristics such as dividing the destination frame into buckets, or with active gates that check the consistency of predicted masks. While these cover a smaller subset of the failure cases, this mode announces itself through a long stretch of empty or inconsistent predictions, and is therefore exploitable for improving the robustness of the pipeline.

\paragraph{Second tier future work.} The second tier of future research directions concerns the paradigm of language as a bridge. We raise three questions. The first concerns how language is represented once written. We currently consume the text embeddings of the VLM and SAM~3 as they come, even though these representations are generic rather than tuned to correspondence, and a representation conditioned on the downstream task might carry the same phrase more usefully. This gain trades against the very property that motivates the paradigm, however, since a text embedding specialised far enough begins to resemble the abstract learned features we set out to avoid. How far language can be specialised before it stops being legible is therefore a question about the paradigm, not only about the pipeline.

The second concerns the ambiguity inherent in language. The same object admits many descriptions, since synonyms and varying expressions can name it differently, and each phrasing may steer the downstream models along a slightly different path. Studying the stochasticity and variability that this introduces into a pipeline like ours would clarify how stable a language bridge can be, with implications for any foundation model or task that takes text as a prompt.

The third is the most fundamental. Our results show that a single phrase reaches within a few IoU points of methods that pass continuous embeddings between their components, which raises the question of which medium compresses object identity more effectively. Language is a discrete channel of low bandwidth that a human can read, whereas a learned embedding of thousands of dimensions carries far more information but remains opaque. Measuring how much is lost in the compression to language, and whether that loss is fundamental or an artefact of current models, would tell us when the legible medium is worth its cost.

\paragraph{Closing thoughts.} This thesis demonstrated the viability of the language as a bridge paradigm, and all the future research paths described above aim at improving and expanding it. Natural language can act as a reliable bridge between machine learning architectures, compressing what a learned latent space would otherwise carry into a phrase a human can read, question, and correct. The beauty of this paradigm lies in how it reconciles two goals that learned correspondence has long traded against each other, reaching the performance of specialised architectures while keeping every step of its reasoning legible. As foundation models continue to absorb a more general understanding of the visual and linguistic world, the most capable systems may increasingly be the ones we compose rather than the ones we train. Language may well remain the simplest and most human medium through which we compose future machine learning architectures while also remaining in the loop as fundamental collaborators.
