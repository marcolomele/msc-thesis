\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis set out to test a single hypothesis, namely that natural language can serve as a bridge between frozen foundation models, and it did so on one of the most demanding settings available, object correspondence across the egocentric and exocentric views of \textit{Ego-Exo4D}. The ``two eyes and one mouth'' pipeline of \cref{chap:pipeline} carries an object between the two viewpoints through a single description that a human can read, with no component trained on the benchmark and every intermediate decision open to inspection. Against the two limitations of prior work named in \cref{chap:intro}, dependence on training and the opacity of learned correspondences, this construction answers both at once. On the official test split it reaches within a few IoU points of O-MaMa, the 2025 challenge winner, and overtakes ObjectRelator, while remaining the only entry in \cref{tab:main} that is at once free of training, agnostic to the camera, and agnostic to the dataset. On the validation analysis, once its failing cases are set aside, its working mode draws level with even LM-EEC, the strongest published method.

The mechanistic analysis of \cref{chap:experiments} does not contradict that hypothesis but instead sharpens it. The deficit to the state of the art is not a uniform blur of imprecision, but a discrete ``dead mass'' that holds roughly a third of cases, and removing it alone would lift the average into LM-EEC territory. The pipeline does not degrade gracefully across the board; it succeeds cleanly on most of the data and fails outright on a locatable minority. That minority is located precisely, since broken down by the quality of the anchor the pipeline draws level with LM-EEC wherever the anchor is good and cedes almost every case wherever it is poor, so the loss is born when the object is first grounded in the destination view rather than when the tracker propagates it. Two factors push a case into the mass, the object's size and the name the model writes for it, and the failure waterfall charges most of the mass to naming alone. Crucially, the legibility of the language bridge is what turned this gap from a verdict into a diagnosis, since a pipeline of frozen and inspectable stages exposes exactly where it breaks, a property no competitor that learns end to end offers. The failures therefore mark where nuance is still missing, not where the paradigm broke down.

Legibility also let us act on the diagnosis directly, and the attempts are as instructive as a clean success would have been. We tested the most obvious repair to the naming bottleneck, conditioning the description on a vocabulary of objects plausible for the scene, and it was the most promising lever of all, lifting accuracy sharply on a small sample, yet the gain did not survive a larger one. We then tightened the grounding by passing the detector's box to the segmenter as a crop, which rescued the cases the pipeline was already failing but corrupted the many it already handled, so an unconditional hint hurts on balance and only a gated one could help. We also ruled out a simpler explanation, since raising the brightness of the often dark egocentric frames did not help, which confirms that the dead mass is a failure of naming and not of raw visibility. None of these closed the gap on its own, but each narrowed where the true fix must act, which is the dividend of a pipeline open to inspection at every stage (\cref{sec:interventions}).

Reading the failure waterfall back through the pipeline turns that diagnosis into four directions for future work. First, the language bridge of \cref{subsec:Stage2} should be constrained to emit a canonical noun phrase while preserving the surrounding scene context the name depends on, because naming dominates the dead mass and the failure is perceptual rather than lexical, with the model nominating the larger neighbour it can actually see when the target shrinks to a fraction of the frame. The obvious remedy of zooming in backfires, since a tight crop destroys the very context that identifies a small object, so the constraint must add discipline to the name without removing the scene around it, and the scene vocabulary conditioning of the previous paragraph is the most direct attempt at exactly this and the clearest candidate for a full evaluation. Second, a stronger grounding model would address the slice of the waterfall where the detector boxes a correctly named object off target, and LocateAnything \cite{wang2026locateanythingfasthighqualityvisionlanguage}, presented in a 2026 technical report from NVIDIA and not yet peer reviewed, is a natural candidate for this stage. Third, an external verification gate is needed to catch the confident mis-groundings that make up the rest of the dead mass, because these failures are silent, the pipeline almost always producing a mask, and its own confidence overlaps almost entirely between hits and misses, so the lesson of the crop hint, that an intervention must fire only on the failing population, applies directly to such a gate. Finally, a trigger that reacquires the object on runs of empty masks would recover the propagation drift that only surfaces once the target is widened beyond the exact failures; unlike the dead mass, this failure announces itself through a long stretch of empty predictions and is therefore exploitable.

These directions all refine a paradigm that already works rather than rescue one that failed. The broader claim the abstract opened therefore holds, that natural language can act as a reliable bridge between machine learning architectures, compressing what a learned latent space would otherwise carry into a phrase a human can read, question, and correct. The beauty of this paradigm lies in how it reconciles two goals that learned correspondence has long traded off against each other, reaching the performance of specialised architectures while keeping every step of its reasoning legible. As foundation models continue to absorb a more general understanding of the visual and linguistic world, the most capable systems may increasingly be the ones we compose rather than the ones we train, and language may remain the simplest and most human medium through which to compose them.
