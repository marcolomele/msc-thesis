\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

\paragraph{Main findings.} This thesis set out to test a single hypothesis: can natural language serve as a bridge between frozen foundation models? To find an answer, the work addressed one of the most demanding settings available in the field of computer vision, object correspondence across the egocentric and exocentric views of \textit{Ego-Exo4D}. The ``Two Eyes and One Mouth'' pipeline, \cref{chap:pipeline}, leverages language as a bridge by mapping an object between two viewpoints through a single textual description, with no component trained on the benchmark. This construction specifically answers the two limitations of prior work named in \cref{chap:intro}, which are dependence on training and the difficulty to explain the correspondences due to the use of complex fusion modules and abstract learned features. On the official test split, the proposed pipeline reaches within a few IoU points of O-MaMa, the 2025 challenge winner, and overtakes ObjectRelator, while remaining the only entry in \cref{tab:main} that is at once free of training, agnostic to the camera, and agnostic to the dataset. On the validation analysis, once its failing cases are set aside, its working mode draws level with even LM-EEC, the strongest published method to date.

The mechanistic analysis of \cref{chap:experiments} does not contradict that hypothesis but instead sharpens it. The deficit to the state of the art is not a uniform blur of imprecision, but a discrete ``dead mass'' that holds roughly a third of cases which point to optimisations to the pipeline rather than insurmountable failures. Because the pipeline relies on a few frames to execute the cross-view correspondence, and then propagates mask predictions with a video tracker, when the bridge fails there is no way to recover nor check the predictions, making the entire take a failure. In fact, when breaking down the results by the quality of the anchor, the pipeline competes with LM-EEC wherever the anchor is good, and cedes almost every case wherever it is poor, meaning that the loss is born at the bridge. The main factors pushing a case into the dead mass are the object's size and the name the VLM writes for it, with the latter issue being the most relevant. Crucially, the legibility of the language bridge is what turned this gap from a verdict into a diagnosis, marking where nuance is still missing, not where the paradigm broke down.

Following the diagnosis, we tested some targeted interventions. The most obvious repair was conditioning the description on a vocabulary of objects plausible for the scene.Yet, the gain did not survive a larger one, suggesting that the issue concenrned more to perception than with naming selection. We also tightened the grounding by passing the detector's box to the segmenter as a crop, which rescued the cases the pipeline was already failing but corrupted the many it already handled, meaning that an unconditional hint hurts on balance and only a gated one could help. We also ruled out a simpler explanation, since raising the brightness of the often dark egocentric frames did not help, which confirms that the dead mass is a failure of naming and not of raw visibility. None of these closed the gap on its own, but each narrowed where the true fix must act, which is the dividend of a pipeline open to inspection at every stage (\cref{sec:interventions}).

\paragraph{Future Work.} First, the language bridge of \cref{subsec:Stage2} should be constrained to emit a canonical noun phrase while preserving the surrounding scene context the name depends on, because naming dominates the dead mass and the failure is perceptual rather than lexical, with the model nominating the larger neighbour it can actually see when the target shrinks to a fraction of the frame. The obvious remedy of zooming in backfires, since a tight crop destroys the very context that identifies a small object, so the constraint must add discipline to the name without removing the scene around it, and the scene vocabulary conditioning of the previous paragraph is the most direct attempt at exactly this and the clearest candidate for a full evaluation. Second, a stronger grounding model would address the slice of the waterfall where the detector boxes a correctly named object off target, and LocateAnything \cite{wang2026locateanythingfasthighqualityvisionlanguage}, presented in a 2026 technical report from NVIDIA and not yet peer reviewed, is a natural candidate for this stage. Third, an external verification gate is needed to catch the confident mis-groundings that make up the rest of the dead mass, because these failures are silent, the pipeline almost always producing a mask, and its own confidence overlaps almost entirely between hits and misses, so the lesson of the crop hint, that an intervention must fire only on the failing population, applies directly to such a gate. Finally, a trigger that reacquires the object on runs of empty masks would recover the propagation drift that only surfaces once the target is widened beyond the exact failures; unlike the dead mass, this failure announces itself through a long stretch of empty predictions and is therefore exploitable.

These directions all refine a paradigm that already works rather than rescue one that failed. The broader claim the abstract opened therefore holds, that natural language can act as a reliable bridge between machine learning architectures, compressing what a learned latent space would otherwise carry into a phrase a human can read, question, and correct. The beauty of this paradigm lies in how it reconciles two goals that learned correspondence has long traded off against each other, reaching the performance of specialised architectures while keeping every step of its reasoning legible. As foundation models continue to absorb a more general understanding of the visual and linguistic world, the most capable systems may increasingly be the ones we compose rather than the ones we train, and language may remain the simplest and most human medium through which to compose them.
