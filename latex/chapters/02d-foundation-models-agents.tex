\section{Foundation Models and Agents}
\label{sec:foundation-models-agents}

The models of \cref{sec:language-grounding} turn a phrase into masks and a mask back
into a phrase, yet neither decides which phrase to ask for nor judges whether the
returned masks are the ones we wanted. Block~3 of our pipeline delegates exactly these
decisions to a foundation model that does not run as a single forward pass but as an
\textit{agent}, a system that reasons about a goal, calls the segmenter of
\cref{subsec:sam3} as a tool, inspects the masks it gets back, and refines its request
until the right object is found. This is the planner role that the agentic interface of
\cref{subsec:sam3} leaves open, and our pipeline fills it with Qwen3.5-35B. To make the
loop precise we first ground what such a model is and what makes its structured outputs
reliable, then formalise the reasoning loop it executes.

\subsection{Large Language Models}
\label{subsec:llm}

A large language model is, at its core, a probability distribution over sequences of
tokens. Given a vocabulary $\set{V}$ and a sequence $x_{1:n}$ drawn from it, the model
factorises the joint probability of the sequence into a product of next-token
conditionals and is trained to maximise the likelihood of observed text, equivalently to
minimise the cross-entropy of each token against the model's prediction of it,

\begin{equation}
  p_\theta(x_{1:n}) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i}),
  \qquad
  \loss = -\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i}).
  \label{eq:autoregressive-lm}
\end{equation}

Here, $x_i$ is the $i$-th token, $x_{<i}$ the tokens that precede it, and
$p_\theta(x_i \mid x_{<i})$ the model's distribution over the next token given that
prefix. Each conditional is produced by the Transformer of \cref{subsec:transformer},
with one modification that enforces the left-to-right factorisation of
\cref{eq:autoregressive-lm}, namely a causal mask applied inside \cref{eq:attention} that
sets to zero every attention weight from a token to its successors, so that position $i$
attends only to $x_{<i}$ and the prediction of $x_i$ cannot see the answer. Because the
keys and values of \cref{eq:attention} for the prefix do not change as generation
proceeds, they are computed once and stored in a key-value cache, which turns generation
of each new token from a recomputation over the whole prefix into a single step against
the cache. This caching is what makes the long contexts our agent needs affordable, since
the loop below accumulates an entire history of reasoning and tool results inside the
context window across many turns.

Trained on next-token likelihood alone, such a model continues text but does not reliably
follow instructions, and scale is what first makes the behaviour worth steering. Kaplan
et al.\ in 2020 \cite{Kaplan2020} and Hoffmann et al.\ in 2022 \cite{Hoffmann2022}
established that loss falls as a power law in parameters, data, and compute, with the
latter showing that compute is best spent by scaling data and parameters together, which
is the reasoning that places a 35-billion-parameter model at a practical frontier for
inference. Raw scale, however, only sharpens the prediction of plausible text; aligning
that capability with instructions is a separate step. Instruction tuning continues
training on pairs of an instruction and its response, converting the next-token predictor
into one that answers a request, and preference alignment then tunes it against human or
model judgements of which response is better, either through reinforcement learning from
human feedback as in Ouyang et al.\ (2022) \cite{Ouyang2022} or through the direct
preference optimisation of Rafailov et al.\ (2023) \cite{Rafailov2023}, which recovers the
same objective without a separate reward model. For our purposes the consequence matters
more than the mechanism. An aligned model emits structured outputs, in particular the JSON
tool calls the agent depends on, far more reliably than a base model, because adherence to
a requested format is precisely the kind of instruction these stages reward.

Our pipeline uses Qwen3.5-35B \cite{qwen35blog} as this aligned model. The Qwen family,
introduced by Bai et al.\ in 2023 \cite{BaiQwen2023}, follows the now standard recipe of
decoder-only Transformers with grouped-query attention, which shares key and value
projections across heads to shrink the cache described above, rotary position embeddings
that encode position by rotating queries and keys rather than by adding a positional
vector, RMSNorm in place of layer normalisation, and SwiGLU feed-forward blocks. Its
multimodal extension, Qwen2-VL \cite{WangQwen2024}, attaches a Vision Transformer of the
kind in \cref{subsec:vit} so that an image enters the same token stream as text, the
property that lets the agent both read a frame and reason over it, and the property
PixelRefer in \cref{subsec:pixelrefer} builds on. The version we deploy adds a
configurable reasoning mode that emits an explicit chain of thought before its answer, a
capability we exploit directly in the loop below.

\subsection{Reasoning and Acting with Tools}
\label{subsec:react}

An aligned model can answer a question in one turn, but segmenting the right object takes
several attempts that each depend on what the previous attempt returned. \textit{ReAct},
proposed by Yao et al.\ in 2023 \cite{Yao2023ReAct}, structures these attempts by
interleaving reasoning with action, extending the observation that eliciting an explicit
chain of thought improves the correctness of a model's answers \cite{Wei2022CoT} from
single answers to a loop that also acts on the world. At step $k$ the agent conditions on
a history $h_k$ and produces a thought $r_k$ and an action $a_k$, the action is executed
to yield an observation $o_k$, and the history is extended with the triple before the next
step,

\begin{equation}
  (r_k, a_k) \sim p_\theta(\,\cdot \mid h_k), \qquad
  o_k = \mathrm{exec}(a_k), \qquad
  h_{k+1} = \mathrm{prune}\big(h_k \,\Vert\, (r_k, a_k, o_k)\big).
  \label{eq:react-loop}
\end{equation}

Here, $h_k$ is the conversation history, beginning with a system prompt that states the
task and the available tools and growing with every turn. The thought $r_k$ is a piece of
natural-language reasoning the model writes for itself, sampled from the distribution
$p_\theta$ of \cref{eq:autoregressive-lm}, and $a_k$ is the action it then commits to. The
operator $\mathrm{exec}$ runs that action outside the model and returns the observation
$o_k$, the symbol $\Vert$ appends the completed triple to the history, and $\mathrm{prune}$
is the bookkeeping we describe shortly. Writing the thought before the action is the whole
point of the loop, because a model that first reasons about which object to segment and why
chooses better actions than one forced to emit a tool call directly, and the trace it
leaves makes each decision legible to us when a run fails.

The action $a_k$ is not free text but a call into a typed action space, defined by a JSON
schema that names each tool and the type of every argument it accepts. Our agent is given
two tools. The first, \texttt{segment\_phrase}, takes a noun phrase and invokes the
promptable concept segmentation of \cref{eq:sam3-pcs} to return candidate masks; the
second, \texttt{examine\_each\_mask}, asks the multimodal model to verify a candidate
against the description of the object being searched for. The model selects a tool and
fills its arguments, the runtime parses the JSON and executes the matching function, and
the result returns as the observation $o_k$. A strongly typed schema is what keeps this
reliable, because constraining the model to a fixed set of tools and argument types leaves
far less room for the hallucinated calls a free-form interface invites, and it is here that
instruction tuning pays off most directly. This supervised contract differs from the
Toolformer paradigm of Schick et al.\ (2023) \cite{Schick2023}, in which a model
discovers from self-supervision when and how to call APIs; our tools and their schema are
fixed in advance, and the agent only decides how to use them.

A loop that grows its history at every step would soon exceed even the long context of
\cref{subsec:llm}, and left unchecked it also drifts into characteristic failures. The
$\mathrm{prune}$ operator of \cref{eq:react-loop} addresses the first problem by keeping
the system prompt and a few anchor turns alongside the most recent tool exchange while
dropping the middle, which bounds the context without discarding the grounding the agent
needs. The failures are of three kinds, namely repeating an action that already failed,
hallucinating arguments outside the schema, and a form of reward hacking in which the agent
settles for a trivially easy phrase rather than the object actually requested. Our loop
guards against each, rejecting a phrase it has already tried, aborting after a run of
consecutive rejections, and degrading gracefully to an empty mask rather than a wrong one,
all within a budget of at most fifteen generations per frame. This is the loop that Block~3
of our pipeline implements, and we trace its behaviour on real frames in
\cref{sec:experiments}.
