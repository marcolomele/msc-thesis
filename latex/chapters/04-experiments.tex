\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}

In the previous chapter we assembled foundation models into our single inference pipeline. This chapter delivers the result. We first describe the experimental set up in which we tested our pipeline. Then, we place our approach against the published state of the art on the official benchmark, asking how far a system that trains nothing can go on a task against competitors that train end-to-end. We then isolate the contribution of each design decision through an ablation that assembles the stages of \cref{sec:method-overview} on one at a time. Finally, we inspect the performance and  give a mechanistic account of where the accuracy is lost. The common thread is that the pipeline succeeds and fails for reasons that are legible, a property no related work offers.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Inference only.}
No component of our pipeline is trained. Every module is taken off the shelf and run in pure inference mode with no finetuning on \textit{Ego-Exo4D} and no supervision specific to the task. This is the asymmetry against which the comparison that follows should be read, since every competitive baseline instead learns a dedicated component on the benchmark itself (\cref{subsec:common-limitations}).

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Following the official Ego-Exo4D Correspondences benchmark \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d} we adopt the Intersection over Union of \cref{eq:iou} as the primary metric, computed between a predicted mask $\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx$ and its ground truth $\mask^D_\frameidx$ on each destination frame and then averaged over every evaluated frame of a take, \cref{eq:iou} in \cref{sec:segmentation}. We report two further metrics. Location Error (LE) is the distance between the centroids of the predicted and ground-truth masks, normalised by the image diagonal, and it measures whether the prediction lands in the right place regardless of its shape. Contour Accuracy (CA), introduced by Perazzi et al. in 2016 \cite{perazzi2016benchmark}, compares the two mask boundaries after translation, so it isolates the quality of the predicted outline from any positional offset. 

\begin{equation}
    \text{LE}(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx, \mask^D_\frameidx) = \frac{\lVert \varsigma(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx) - \varsigma(\mask^D_\frameidx) \rVert_2}{d}
    \quad \quad
    \text{CA}(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx, \mask^D_\frameidx) = \frac{2 \cdot P \cdot R}{P + R}
    \label{eq:le-ca}
\end{equation}

where $\varsigma(\cdot)$ denotes the centroid of a mask and $d = \sqrt{H^2 + W^2}$ is the image diagonal for a frame of height $H$ and width $W$, and $P$ and $R$ are the precision and recall computed by matching points on the predicted boundary $\partial\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx$ to points on the ground-truth boundary $\partial\mask^D_\frameidx$ within a tolerance after aligning centroids, following \cite{perazzi2016benchmark}.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
We set $\abs{\set{S}} = 1$ seed frame and $\abs{\set{A}} = 3$ anchor frames, since a single seed frame already yields a reliable description while three anchors balance the stability of propagation against its cost, as the ablation of \cref{sec:ablations} confirms. We select anchor frames in Stage~3 with Grounding DINO \cite{liu2024groundingdino}, keeping language as the sole bridge across views, and we use Qwen~3.5~35B \cite{qwen35blog} as the VLM backend for both Stage~2 and the SAM~3 agent loop (\cref{subsec:Stage2}). The entire system is implemented in Python with PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries, and its inference is run on a single NVIDIA H200 Graphics Processing Unit with 80GB of Video Random Access Memory.

\textbf{Results.} \Cref{tab:main} reports results on the Ego-Exo4D Correspondences v2 test split. The gap to the current state of the art is large and was expected. By the time of writing, LM-EEC of Hu et al. \cite{hu2025lmeec} holds the strongest published numbers, scoring 54.98 and 65.77 IoU in the \textit{Ego2Exo} and \textit{Exo2Ego} directions, against our 37.7 and 40.6. A method that retrains the full SAM~2 backbone on the benchmark videos has access to a signal our pipeline by default lacks and can only approximate with distribution it learned in prior training. 

The gap flattens once it is restricted to methods that do not retrain a segmentation backbone. Our approach trails O-MaMa \cite{o-mama}, winner of the 2025 Ego-Exo4D Correspondences Challenge, by roughly five IoU points in both directions ($-11.5\%$ on \textit{Ego2Exo}, $-7.9\%$ on \textit{Exo2Ego}), while surpassing ObjectRelator \cite{fu2025objectrelator} in IoU across both settings (37.7 against 35.3 on \textit{Ego2Exo}, 40.6 against 40.3 on \textit{Exo2Ego}). Remarking that our pipeline is training-free closes to within a few points of the challenge winner, and overtakes a method built on jointly learned cross-view alignment, suggests that much of what the learned components contribute can be recovered by composing strong foundational models around the language bridge.

The other error metrics provide further detail about our pipeline. In the \textit{Ego2Exo} direction, our LE of 0.071 is worse than that of XMem+XSegTx (0.038), a baseline that operates within a single view, even though our IoU is higher. The pipeline therefore tends to recover the correct object region while placing it with coarser spatial precision than its competitors. This profile reflects grounding the object through a language description rather than a learned correspondence, creating a distinction also in the errors of mask matching made by ObjectRelator and O-MaMa and consistent with (\cref{subsec:Stage3}).
