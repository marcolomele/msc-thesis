\chapter{Experiments}
\label{chap:experiments}

In the previous chapter we assembled foundation models into our single inference pipeline. This chapter delivers the result. We first describe the experimental set up in which we tested our pipeline. Then, we place our approach against the published state of the art on the official benchmark, asking how far a system that trains nothing can go on a task against competitors that train end-to-end. We then isolate the contribution of each design decision through an ablation that assembles the stages of \cref{sec:method-overview} one at a time. We then inspect the performance and give a mechanistic account of where the accuracy is lost. Finally, we run a set of quick interventions aimed at the bottlenecks that the account surfaces, which reveal the problem to be more nuanced than any single fix. The common thread is that the pipeline succeeds and fails for reasons that are legible and potentially directly addressable.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

\paragraph{Inference only.}
No component of our pipeline is trained. Every module is taken off the shelf and run in pure inference mode with no finetuning on \textit{Ego-Exo4D} and no supervision specific to the task. This is the asymmetry against which the comparison that follows should be read, since every competitive baseline instead learns a dedicated component on the benchmark itself.

\paragraph{Evaluation.}
Following the official benchmark \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d}, we adopt the Intersection over Union of \cref{eq:iou} as the primary metric, computed between a predicted mask $\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx$ and its ground truth $\mask^D_\frameidx$ on each destination frame and then averaged over every evaluated frame of a take, \cref{eq:iou} in \cref{sec:segmentation}. We report two further metrics. Location Error (LE) is the distance between the centroids of the predicted and ground-truth masks, normalised by the image diagonal, and it measures whether the prediction lands in the right place regardless of its shape. Contour Accuracy (CA) \cite{perazzi2016benchmark} measures how closely the predicted outline traces the ground-truth boundary.

\begin{equation}
    \text{LE}(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx, \mask^D_\frameidx) = \frac{\lVert \varsigma(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx) - \varsigma(\mask^D_\frameidx) \rVert_2}{d}
    \quad \quad
    \text{CA}(\hat{\mask}^D_\frameidx, \mask^D_\frameidx) = \frac{2 \cdot P \cdot R}{P + R}
    \label{eq:le-ca}
\end{equation}

where $\varsigma(\cdot)$ is the centroid of a mask, $d = \sqrt{H^2 + W^2}$ the image diagonal of a frame of height $H$ and width $W$, and $P$ and $R$ the boundary precision and recall obtained by matching the predicted contour to the ground-truth contour within a tolerance \cite{perazzi2016benchmark}.


\paragraph{Implementation.}
We set $\abs{\set{S}} = 1$ seed frame and $\abs{\set{A}} = 3$ anchor frames, since a single seed frame already yields a reliable description while three anchors balance the stability of propagation against its cost, as the ablation of \cref{sec:ablations} confirms. We select anchor frames in Stage~3 with Grounding DINO \cite{liu2024groundingdino}, keeping language as the sole bridge across views, and we use Qwen~3.5~35B \cite{qwen35blog} as the VLM backend for both Stage~2 and the SAM~3 agent loop (\cref{subsec:Stage2}). The entire system is implemented in Python with PyTorch and Hugging Face libraries, and its inference is run on a single NVIDIA H200 Graphics Processing Unit with 80GB of Video Random Access Memory.

\textbf{Results.} \Cref{tab:main} reports results on the Ego-Exo4D Correspondences v2 test split. The gap to the current state of the art is large and was expected. By the time of writing, LM-EEC of Hu et al. \cite{hu2025lmeec} holds the strongest published numbers, scoring 54.98 and 65.77 IoU in the \textit{Ego2Exo} and \textit{Exo2Ego} directions, against our 37.7 and 40.6. A method that retrains the full SAM~2 backbone on the benchmark videos has access to a signal our pipeline by default lacks and can only approximate with distribution it learned in prior training. 

The gap flattens once it is restricted to methods that do not retrain a segmentation backbone. Our approach trails O-MaMa \cite{o-mama}, winner of the 2025 Ego-Exo4D Correspondences Challenge, by roughly five IoU points in both directions ($-11.5\%$ on \textit{Ego2Exo}, $-7.9\%$ on \textit{Exo2Ego}), while surpassing ObjectRelator \cite{fu2025objectrelator} in IoU across both settings (37.7 against 35.3 on \textit{Ego2Exo}, 40.6 against 40.3 on \textit{Exo2Ego}). That our pipeline, while training-free, closes to within a few points of the challenge winner and overtakes a method built on jointly learned cross-view alignment suggests that much of what the learned components contribute can be recovered by composing strong foundation models around the language bridge.

The other error metrics provide further detail about our pipeline. In the \textit{Ego2Exo} direction, our LE of 0.071 is worse than that of XMem+XSegTx (0.038), a baseline that operates within a single view, even though our IoU is higher. The pipeline therefore tends to recover the correct object region while placing it with coarser spatial precision than its competitors. This profile reflects grounding the object through a language description rather than a learned correspondence, a distinction from the mask matching errors of ObjectRelator and O-MaMa that is consistent with \cref{subsec:Stage3}. Qualitative examples for successful cases are displayed in \cref{fig:successful-exmaples}.

\paragraph{Performance against LM-EEC.} We compare the full pipeline against LM-EEC \cite{hu2025lmeec} on 30\% of the validation takes in the \textit{Exo2Ego} direction, counting a segmentation as successful when its IoU exceeds 0.5. \Cref{fig:pipeline-lmeec} summarises the comparison from four angles on the same runs. The IoU distribution in the top left panel is bimodal, with the first mode at zero and second mode above 0.8 where the pipeline matches or exceeds LM-EEC. The zero mass marks the cases that fail outright, and is what drags our mean to 40.5 below LM-EEC's 64.4. Once the cases each system fails outright are removed, by keeping only an IoU above 0.2, our approach levels the SOTA, at 73.2 for the pipeline against 70.5 for LM-EEC. Recovering the dead mass would therefore significantly lift the mean IoU, which we expand on in \cref{sec:gap-analysis}.

The same split recurs whenever we slice the data. By scenario, the strongest regimes are basketball (81\% joint success) and music (65\%), whereas bike repair is the weakest at 26\%, with both methods failing on 34\% of its runs. Bike repair concentrates small objects whose identity is ambiguous to name, which is precisely where the language bridge of Stage~2 struggles to carry the object across views. Object size tells the same story, since joint success holds near 34\% on small and 29\% on medium objects and then jumps to 62\% on large ones, while the rate at which both methods fail drops from 38\% to 5\%. LM-EEC tracks the same pattern, so the difficulty is shared rather than specific to our pipeline. Interestingly, the small and medium regimes are almost identical, which suggests the difficulty sets in below a size threshold rather than scaling smoothly with it.

Crucially, the breakdown by anchor IoU locates the failure. When the anchor IoU is high (in $[0.7,1]$), both methods succeed jointly in 80\% of cases and LM-EEC's advantage shrinks to 7\%. When it is low (in $[0,0.3)$), the pipeline collapses to 5\% joint success while LM-EEC alone wins 60\% of those cases. The state of the art therefore dominates exactly where our anchors are poor, which marks anchor selection and object description as the directions with the highest leverage for improvement.

What remains is to ablate every component of the pipeline to understand its contribution, \cref{sec:ablations}, and then inspect the failure cases to explain the gap to LM-EEC, \cref{sec:gap-analysis}.
