\section{Language Grounding}
\label{sec:language-grounding}

The models of \cref{sec:segmentation} segment what a user points at, yet
our pipeline must segment what a user names. A point or a box specifies a location; a
noun phrase specifies a concept, and turning the words ``the blue ceramic mug'' into the
pixels that depict it is the problem of grounding language in vision. This section builds
that capability in the order the pipeline assembles it. We begin with the alignment of
images and text in a shared space, narrow from whole-image alignment to detection that
localises a phrase to a region, extend localisation into the segmentation and tracking of
every instance of a concept, and close with the inverse operation that turns a mask back
into the discriminative phrase that names it. Throughout, we reserve the symbol $t$ for a
text prompt and write $g_\text{txt}$ and $g_\text{img}$ for the text and image encoders
that map either modality into a common vector space.

\subsection{Aligning Vision and Language}
\label{subsec:multimodality}

Grounding rests on a shared representation in which an image and a sentence describing it
land close together. \textit{CLIP}, introduced by Radford et al.\ in 2021
\cite{radford2021clip}, learns exactly this from raw supervision that requires no manual
labelling, namely the four hundred million image and caption pairs already paired on the
web. It couples two encoders, an image encoder $g_\text{img}$ that is the Vision
Transformer of \cref{subsec:vit} and a text encoder $g_\text{txt}$ that is a Transformer
of the kind in \cref{subsec:transformer}, each producing a single descriptor that is then
$L_2$-normalised so that dot products become the cosine similarities of \cref{subsec:dino}.
The two encoders are trained jointly to agree on matching pairs and disagree on the rest.

Given a batch of $N$ pairs, the model scores every image against every caption and is
asked to recover which caption belongs to which image. Writing $\cossim{\cdot}{\cdot}$ for
the cosine similarity of two normalised descriptors, the symmetric contrastive objective is

\begin{equation}
  \loss
  = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \left[
      \log\frac{\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
               {\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_j)}/\kappa\big)}
      +
      \log\frac{\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
               {\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_j)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
    \right].
  \label{eq:clip-infonce}
\end{equation}

Here, $\img_i$ is the $i$-th image and $t_i$ its true caption, so the diagonal pairs
$(\img_i,t_i)$ are the positives and every off-diagonal pairing is a negative. The first
logarithm reads each row of the similarity matrix as a distribution over captions and
rewards placing the mass on the correct one; the second reads each column as a
distribution over images and does the same in the other direction, which is why the loss
is averaged over both.\footnote{The original work calls the temperature $\tau$; we write
$\kappa$ throughout to keep $\tau$ for the video frame index introduced in
\cref{subsec:sam}.} The temperature $\kappa$ scales the similarities before the softmax,
controlling how sharply the model separates the positive from its negatives. This is the
\textit{InfoNCE} loss, and minimising it pulls each image toward its caption and pushes it
away from the $N-1$ others in the batch, so that after training a single image embedding
can be compared against the embedding of any phrase. Classification then needs no
task-specific head, because an unseen category is recognised by embedding its name as text
and taking the nearest caption, which is the property that makes the alignment open
vocabulary.

CLIP aligns whole images with whole sentences, and this is precisely its limit for
grounding. A high similarity tells us that a mug appears somewhere in the frame but not
where, because the descriptor has discarded spatial structure in the course of summarising
the image. Two lines of work extend the alignment toward the pixels. Visual instruction
tuning, introduced as \textit{LLaVA} by Liu et al.\ in 2023 \cite{liu2023llava}, keeps a
frozen CLIP image encoder and trains only a lightweight projection that feeds visual
tokens into a large language model, so that an image becomes an input any instruction
follower can reason over in natural language, a design we return to in
\cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}. Earlier, Flamingo had shown that interleaving image
and text tokens lets a single model condition on several images at once
\cite{Alayrac2022}. Both make the language richer; neither recovers the location a mask
requires, which is the gap the next model closes.

\subsection{Open-Vocabulary Detection with Grounding DINO}
\label{subsec:groundingdino}

Localising a phrase to a region is the task of detection, reframed so that the set of
recognisable objects is not fixed in advance. Classical detectors predict from a closed
list of categories baked into a classification head. The \textit{DETR} family removed much
of the surrounding machinery, with Carion et al.\ in 2020 \cite{carion2020detr} casting
detection as the direct prediction of a set of boxes by a Transformer with learnable
object queries, matched to ground truth by a bipartite assignment we do not derive here,
and the DINO detector of Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2023dinodet} sharpening its training and
convergence. The closed head, however, still ties each query to a fixed vocabulary, so the
detector cannot name an object it was never trained on.

\textit{Grounding DINO}, presented by Liu et al.\ in 2024 \cite{liu2024groundingdino},
breaks this constraint by fusing a detector with the language alignment of
\cref{subsec:multimodality}. It pairs an image backbone with a text backbone and lets the
two modalities read each other before any box is proposed. Writing $\mm{X}_\text{img}$ for
the image tokens and $\mm{X}_\text{txt}$ for the tokens of the prompt, a cross-modal
feature enhancer applies the cross-attention of \cref{subsec:transformer} in both
directions,

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mm{X}}_\text{img} = \mm{X}_\text{img} + \attn(\mm{X}_\text{img}, \mm{X}_\text{txt}, \mm{X}_\text{txt}),
  \qquad
  \tilde{\mm{X}}_\text{txt} = \mm{X}_\text{txt} + \attn(\mm{X}_\text{txt}, \mm{X}_\text{img}, \mm{X}_\text{img}),
  \label{eq:gdino-enhancer}
\end{equation}

so that each image token is rewritten with the words it is most compatible with and each
word with the image regions it most refers to. In \cref{eq:gdino-enhancer} the image
tokens act as the queries of \cref{eq:attention} while the text tokens supply the keys and
values, and the residual additions preserve the original features alongside their
cross-modal refinements. From the enhanced image tokens the model selects those most
similar to the prompt to initialise its object queries, a step that focuses detection on
language rather than on a fixed category set, and a cross-modal decoder then emits for each
query a box together with its alignment to the phrase. Because the score a region receives
is its similarity to arbitrary text rather than the activation of a class neuron, the same
detector localises any noun phrase, which is what makes it open vocabulary. The pipeline
exploits this directly. Running Grounding DINO with a deliberately permissive confidence
threshold turns its per-region alignment into a signal for ranking candidate destination
frames by how strongly the named object is present, a use of detection as a frame score
rather than as a final answer that we develop in \cref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Promptable Concept Segmentation with SAM 3}
\label{subsec:sam3}

A box around a phrase is still coarser than the mask our pipeline carries, and Grounding
DINO localises one region at a time rather than every instance of a concept at once.
\textit{SAM 3}, presented by Ravi et al.\ in 2025 \cite{ravi2025sam3}, closes both gaps by
defining \textit{promptable concept segmentation}, the task of detecting, segmenting, and
tracking all instances of a concept named by a noun phrase, image exemplars, or both. It
extends the promptable design of \cref{eq:sam-decoder} by admitting a text prompt where
SAM accepted only a point or a box. Encoding the phrase with a text encoder $g_\text{txt}$
in the manner of \cref{subsec:multimodality}, the model returns not a single mask but the
set of masks that realise the concept,

\begin{equation}
  \{\mask_1, \dots, \mask_k\} = \text{SAM\,3}\big(\img,\, g_\text{txt}(t)\big),
  \label{eq:sam3-pcs}
\end{equation}

where $\img$ is the image, $t$ the noun phrase, and each $\mask_j$ delineates one instance
matching $t$. The shift from \cref{eq:sam-decoder} is that the prompt is now semantic, so
the number $k$ of returned masks is itself an output rather than a fixed choice among the
three candidates SAM produced for an ambiguous click. To segment a concept reliably the
model must both decide which regions belong to it and separate one instance from the next,
and SAM 3 couples a detector to a segmenter so that recognition and delineation are learned
together. Its video module reuses the streaming memory of SAM 2 from \cref{subsec:sam},
propagating each discovered mask across frames through the same memory attention, a reuse
that lets the concept be tracked once it is named, a capability covered fully in \cref{sec:addition-of-time}.

A noun phrase, however expressive, struggles with the relational descriptions that
cluttered scenes demand. Asking for ``the mug left of the laptop'' or ``the bottle nearest
the hand'' presses against what a short phrase can encode, because the target is defined by
its relation to other objects rather than by its own appearance. To reach these cases SAM 3
exposes an agentic interface in which a multimodal language model acts as a planner that
treats segmentation as a tool. The planner proposes a simple phrase, invokes
\cref{eq:sam3-pcs} to obtain candidate masks, inspects the result against the original
request, and refines its query, repeating until the masks satisfy the description. This
loop decomposes a relational expression into discriminative sub-queries that the segmenter
can handle one at a time, and it is the exact mechanism on which Block 3 of our pipeline is
built, with the planner role filled by the multimodal model of
\cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}. The interface also explains why short, discriminative
phrases matter more than verbose ones. A canonical phrase such as ``blue ceramic mug''
grounds more accurately than a long sentence, which is why the planner's task is to distil
a rich description into a compact prompt rather than to elaborate it, a finding that
directly shapes the prompt design we adopt in our pipeline.

\subsection{Mask-Conditioned Description}
\label{subsec:pixelrefer}

Segmentation turns a phrase into a mask; the agentic loop above also needs the inverse,
turning a mask back into a phrase so the planner can judge whether the right object was
found. This is the distinction between referring expression comprehension, which locates
the region a phrase denotes, and referring expression generation, which is given a region
and must produce an expression that identifies it uniquely. Generation is the harder of the
two, because a useful expression must be discriminative rather than merely accurate; naming
a region ``a mug'' is correct yet useless when three mugs share the scene.

\textit{PixelRefer}, introduced by Yuan et al.\ in 2025
\cite{yuan2025pixelreferunifiedframeworkspatiotemporal}, performs this generation by
conditioning a multimodal model on the pixels of a mask. It builds on a Qwen2-VL backbone,
the vision and language model of \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}, and injects the
binary mask alongside the image so that the model attends to the indicated region while
describing it, producing text grounded in the specific instance rather than the scene as a
whole. In the pipeline it provides an alternative source of the object description that
seeds the search for correspondences, a role we compare against the multimodal model in
\cref{sec:experiments}.

The descriptions PixelRefer generates are useful only if they can be compared, and the
agentic loop produces far more candidate masks than the expensive multimodal verifier can
afford to inspect. A cheap semantic filter solves this. \textit{Sentence-BERT}, proposed by
Reimers and Gurevych in 2019 \cite{reimers2019sentencebert}, adapts a BERT encoder so that
a whole sentence collapses into a single vector by mean-pooling its token embeddings,
trained so that cosine similarity in this space tracks semantic agreement. Encoding both
the source description and each candidate description with such an encoder $g_\text{sbert}$,
the pipeline keeps the candidate whose meaning is closest to the source,

\begin{equation}
  \hat{\jmath} = \arg\max_{j}\, \cossim{g_\text{sbert}(t_\text{src})}{g_\text{sbert}(t_j)},
  \label{eq:rerank}
\end{equation}

where $t_\text{src}$ is the description of the object being searched for, each $t_j$ is the
description of a candidate mask, and $\cossim{\cdot}{\cdot}$ is again the cosine similarity
of \cref{subsec:dino}. The compact encoder we use is distilled by the attention-transfer of
\textit{MiniLM} \cite{wang2020minilm}, which compresses a large Transformer into a model
small enough to score many candidates at negligible cost while retaining most of its
similarity signal. \Cref{eq:rerank} therefore acts as a pre-filter, discarding
implausible masks by language alone before the multimodal model is asked to verify the few
that survive, an ordering that keeps the agentic loop of \cref{subsec:sam3} affordable.

Taken together, the four capabilities of this section form one path from words to pixels
and back. Alignment makes images and phrases comparable, detection localises a phrase to a
region, concept segmentation expands that region into the masks of every matching instance
and tracks them through time, and mask-conditioned description closes the loop by naming
what was found. The agent that orchestrates this path, and the foundation model that fills
the planner's role, are the subject of \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}.
