\section{Language Grounding}
\label{sec:language-grounding}

The models described in \cref{sec:segmentation} segment what a user ``points'' at. In contrast,
our pipeline is driven by language, and therefore it must segment what a user ``describes''. While both modalities act as localization signal, their treatment requires a complete paradigm switch from geometry to semantics. In this section, we explore language grounding, a concept broadly defined as the mapping of natural language expressions to the image pixels that show what they name. In other words, we aim at turning a phrase such as ``red cherry tomatoes'' into the precise mask that delineates it. We begin with the alignment of images and text in a shared space, then narrow to detection that localises a phrase to a region, and finally  reach text-driven segmentation and tracking with SAM 3. Throughout, we reserve the symbol $t$ for a text prompt and write $g_\text{txt}$ and $g_\text{img}$ for the text and image encoders that map either modality into a shared vector space.

\subsection{Aligning Vision and Language}
\label{subsec:multimodality}

Grounding rests on a shared representation in which an image and a sentence describing it
land close together. \textit{CLIP}, introduced by Radford et al.\ in 2021
\cite{radford2021clip}, learns exactly this from raw supervision that requires no manual
labelling by training on four hundred million image and caption pairs from the web. It couples two encoders, an image encoder $g_\text{img}$ that is the Vision Transformer of \cref{subsec:vit} and a text encoder $g_\text{txt}$ that is a Transformer of the kind in \cref{subsec:transformer}, each producing a single descriptor that is then
$L_2$-normalised so that dot products become the cosine similarities of \cref{subsec:dino}.
The two encoders are trained jointly to agree on matching pairs and disagree on the rest.

Given a batch of $N$ pairs, the model scores every image against every caption and is
asked to recover which caption belongs to which image. Writing $\cossim{\cdot}{\cdot}$ for
the cosine similarity of two normalised descriptors, the symmetric contrastive objective is

\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  \loss
  = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \Bigg[
      &\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
               {\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_j)}/\kappa\big)}
      \\
      +\,&\log\frac{\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_i)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
               {\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp\!\big(\cossim{g_\text{img}(\img_j)}{g_\text{txt}(t_i)}/\kappa\big)}
    \Bigg].
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:clip-infonce}
\end{equation}

Here, $\img_i$ is the $i$-th image and $t_i$ its true caption, making the diagonal pairs
$(\img_i,t_i)$ are the positives and every off-diagonal pairing is a negative. The first
logarithm reads each row of the similarity matrix as a distribution over captions and
rewards placing the mass on the correct one; the second reads each column as a
distribution over images and does the same in the other direction, which is why the loss
is averaged over both.\footnote{The original work calls the temperature $\tau$; we write
$\kappa$ to keep $\tau$ for the video frame index, \cref{subsec:sam}.} The temperature $\kappa$ scales the similarities before the softmax,
controlling how sharply the model separates the positive from its negatives. This is the
\textit{InfoNCE} loss \cite{rusak2025infonceidentifyinggaptheory}, and minimising it pulls each image toward its caption and pushes it away from the others in the batch, so that after training a single image embedding
can be compared against the embedding of any phrase. 

Classification then needs no head specific to the task, because an unseen category is recognised by embedding its name as text and taking the nearest caption, which is what makes the alignment open vocabulary. However, CLIP's limit is that this alignment operates at the level of whole images and whole
sentences; a high similarity indicates that a concept is present in the frame but reveals nothing about its location, since all spatial structure is lost when compressing the image into a single semantic-rich embedding.

Two lines of work extend the alignment toward the pixels, namely visual instruction
tuning and multimodal token interleaving. Visual instruction tuning, introduced as
\textit{LLaVA} by Liu et al.\ in 2023 \cite{liu2023llava}, keeps a frozen CLIP image
encoder and trains only a lightweight projection that feeds visual tokens into a large
language model, so that an image becomes an input any instruction follower can reason over
in natural language, a design we return to in \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}.
Multimodal token interleaving, introduced by Alayrac et al.\ in 2022 as \textit{Flamingo}
\cite{Alayrac2022}, lets a single model condition on several images at once by
interleaving image and text tokens in a shared sequence. Both make the language richer with the integration of information from images, but
neither recovers the location a mask requires.


\subsection{Grounding DINO}
\label{subsec:groundingdino}

The task of localising a phrase to an image's region is equivalent to open-vocabulary detection. Open-vocabulary means that an object can be detected regardless of whether it was shown at training time. Classical detectors fail in this regard, with each region proposal being compared against a closed list of categories. The DETR family  \cite{carion2020detr} designed a Transformer that
predicts boxes directly, casting detection as the prediction of a set of boxes through learnable object queries matched to ground truth by a bipartite assignment. \cite{zhang2023dinodet} further
sharpen the training and convergence with a DINO-based detector. Nonetheless, the vocabulary constraint
persist; the detector cannot name an object it has never seen before.

\textit{Grounding DINO} (G-DINO), presented by Liu et al.\ in 2024 \cite{liu2024groundingdino},
breaks this constraint by fusing a detector with the language alignment of
\cref{subsec:multimodality}. It pairs an image backbone with a text backbone and lets the
two modalities read each other before any box is proposed. Letting $\mm{X}_\text{img}$ for
the image tokens and $\mm{X}_\text{txt}$ for the tokens of the prompt, G-DINO applies the cross-attention of \cref{subsec:transformer} in both directions as a feature enhancer,

\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\mm{X}}_\text{img} = \mm{X}_\text{img} + \attn(\mm{X}_\text{img}, \mm{X}_\text{txt}, \mm{X}_\text{txt}),
  \qquad
  \tilde{\mm{X}}_\text{txt} = \mm{X}_\text{txt} + \attn(\mm{X}_\text{txt}, \mm{X}_\text{img}, \mm{X}_\text{img}),
  \label{eq:gdino-enhancer}
\end{equation}

so that each image token is rewritten with the words it is most compatible with and each
word with the image regions it most closely refers to. In \cref{eq:gdino-enhancer} the image
tokens act as the queries of \cref{eq:attention} while the text tokens supply the keys and
values. Additionally, G-DINO leverages residual additions to preserve the original features alongside their
refinements across modalities. 

Having produced the enhanced image tokens, the model selects those most
similar to the prompt to initialise its object queries. This  focuses detection on
language rather than on a fixed category set. Next, a multimodal decoder then emits for each
query a box together with its alignment to the phrase.  Because the score a region receives
is its similarity to arbitrary text rather than the activation of a class neuron, the same
detector localises any noun phrase, making it open vocabulary by design. 

Finally, G-DINO output is a bounding box, localizing one phrase at a time. Our pipeline exploits the
alignment signal directly. We run Grounding DINO with a deliberately permissive confidence
threshold and exploit the alignment score as a proxy for how likely the named object is present in each frame, which then allows us to rank frames in the destination view ( \cref{sec:experiments}).  While G-DINO surpasses the hurdle of open-vocabulary detection, it still does not achieve segmentation. 

\subsection{Segment Anything Model 3}
\label{subsec:sam3}

SAM 3, presented by Ravi et al.\ in 2025 \cite{ravi2025sam3} define the \textit{promptable concept segmentation} task. Its goal is to detect, segment, and track all instances of a concept named by a noun phrase, image exemplars, or both. The model adopts the Perception Encoder \cite{bolya2025perceptionencoder}, a recent vision-language backbone, the memory modules of SAM 2, and three innovations: a detector conditioned on the text prompt, a global presence token, and a coupling between the detector and the tracker that prevents mask drift. Together these let the model both decide which regions belong to a concept and delineate every instance, rather than waiting for a user to specify one. In essence, SAM3 receives as input a textual noun phrase and has been trained to returns the set of all masks that realise the concept,

\begin{equation}
  \{\mask_1, \dots, \mask_k\} = \text{SAM\,3}\big(\img,\, g_\text{txt}(t)\big),
  \label{eq:sam3-pcs}
\end{equation}

where $\img$ is the image, $t$ the noun phrase, and each $\mask_j$ delineates one instance in $\img$
matching $t$. The number $k$ of returned masks is itself an output rather than a fixed
choice, because the model has the freedom to decide how many objects in the image are related to the described concept.

\paragraph{Detector with Text Conditioning.} SAM 3 introduces a detector built on the DETR architecture whose image features are conditioned on the text prompt before any box is proposed. A fusion
encoder takes the image tokens produced by the Perception Encoder and the encoded phrase $g_\text{txt}(t)$
and applies cross-attention following  \cref{eq:gdino-enhancer}. This produces image
representations informed by the text, from which the detector initialises its object
queries. Each query then scores its proposed region by its alignment to the phrase. The
detector is supervised jointly with two complementary losses; first, a standard detection loss
from the DAC-DETR architecture \cite{hu2023dacdetr}, and second, an alignment loss between each proposed region and the textual concept. This joint supervision is what ensures that recognition and localisation are
learned together rather than in sequence. 

\paragraph{Presence Token.} The most significant architectural innovation is a learned
global presence token that decouples recognition from localisation. In a standard detector,
each object query must simultaneously decide whether a concept is present and locate it,
which conflates two distinct problems and degrades performance when the concept is absent
from the frame. To this end, SAM 3 separates them. On the one hand, the presence token attends over the full image and
returns a scalar score $s^\text{pres}$ indicating whether the queried concept exists at
all. On the other hand, the object queries are responsible only for localising the instances they have
been guided toward. The final confidence for each detected instance is the product of the
object query's own score and the presence score,

\begin{equation}
  s_j = s_j^\text{query} \cdot s^\text{pres},
  \label{eq:sam3-presence}
\end{equation}

so that no query can fire on an absent concept even if it finds a locally plausible region.
This gate substantially reduces false detections in frames where the object is not visible,
is the dominant failure mode when searching in a long video for a concept present only
in few isolated frames.

\paragraph{Detector and Tracker Integration.} SAM 3's video module inherits the streaming
memory of SAM 2, with memory attention, a memory bank, and an occlusion head operating as
in \cref{eq:mem-attn}. The innovation is in how the detector and the tracker are coupled
across time. Rather than propagating indefinitely from the last reliable mask, the tracker
is periodically re-anchored by the detections that the detector produces on each new frame, correcting the tracker's current estimates before error can accumulate. This coupling addresses the drift problem hidden in \cref{eq:mem-attn}, making promptable concept segmentation viable over long clips, thus acting as the segmentation engine of our pipeline.

\paragraph{SAM 3 Agent.} One crucial limitation of SAM 3 is that it was trained on maximum 32-token noun phrases. However expressive, these short statements struggles with complex relational descriptions and thus limits SAM 3's applicability. To this end, SAM 3's authors design an agentic loop in which a multimodal language model (MLLM) acts as a planner that orchestrates complex segmentations and treats SAM 3 as a tool. The planner proposes a simple phrase, invokes \cref{eq:sam3-pcs} to obtain candidate masks, inspects the result against the original request, and refines its query, repeating until the masks satisfy the description. This loop decomposes a relational expression into discriminative sub-queries that the segmenter can handle one at a time.

Taken together, the three capabilities of this section form one path from words to pixels.
Alignment makes images and phrases comparable, detection localises a phrase to a region,
and concept segmentation expands that region into the masks of every matching instance and
tracks them through time. To better understand what is an agentic loop and how a MLLM can orchestrates segmentation, we briefly overview the large language models and agent design in the next section \cref{sec:foundation-models-agents}.
