\section{Cross-View Object Correspondence Related Works}
\label{sec:related-works}

\subsection{Feature Matching and Dense Correspondence}
\label{subsec:matching}

Carrying a mask from one camera to another is segmentation given a second view, and it
turns on a prior question, namely which point in one image corresponds to which point in
the other. Correspondence was first approached through handcrafted local descriptors.
The \textit{SIFT} descriptor of Lowe in 2004 \cite{lowe2004sift}, followed by
\textit{SURF} \cite{bay2008surf} and \textit{ORB} \cite{rublee2011orb}, detects a sparse
set of keypoints and encodes the neighbourhood of each into a vector built to be
invariant to scale and rotation; two images are then matched by pairing keypoints whose
descriptors are nearest in this space. These methods remain remarkably durable, but
their invariances are designed rather than learned, and they degrade sharply under the
wide changes in viewpoint and illumination that separate an egocentric from an
exocentric camera.

Learning replaced design in two stages. SuperPoint \cite{detone2018superpoint} trains a
single network to detect keypoints and emit descriptors jointly, and SuperGlue
\cite{sarlin2020superglue} matches two such sets with an attention-based graph network
that reasons about all candidate pairs at once rather than matching each keypoint in
isolation. LoFTR \cite{sun2021loftr} then dispensed with detection altogether, applying
the self-attention and cross-attention of \cref{subsec:transformer} between two images to
produce dense matches directly, so that correspondences can be recovered even in the
textureless regions where keypoint detectors find nothing to anchor to.

RoMa, introduced by Edstedt et al.\ in 2024 \cite{roma}, is the model the pipeline
adopts, and it carries the dense paradigm to its robust extreme. Rather than a list of
matched points, RoMa predicts a dense \textit{warp field} $\warp$ together with a
per-coordinate confidence,

\begin{equation}
  (\warp, c) = f_\theta(\img_a, \img_b),
  \qquad
  \warp(\vv{x}) \in \R^2,
  \quad
  c(\vv{x}) \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:warp}
\end{equation}

where $f_\theta$ is the matcher applied to a source image $\img_a$ and a target image
$\img_b$. For every coordinate $\vv{x}$ in the source, the warp $\warp(\vv{x})$ returns
the coordinate in the target that it judges to correspond, so that $\warp$ is a complete
deformation carrying one image onto the other, and the confidence $c(\vv{x})$ reports how
reliable that single match is, near one where the geometry is certain and near zero where
the source point is occluded or has no counterpart. RoMa estimates the field coarsely
from the self-supervised features of \cref{subsec:dino}, which give it a semantic anchor
that survives wide changes in viewpoint, and then refines it locally to pixel precision.
The confidence is what makes the warp field usable as evidence rather than a raw
prediction; the pipeline ranks candidate frames by the number of confident matches whose
source coordinate falls inside the object mask $\mask$, turning \cref{eq:warp} into a
language-free signal for selecting where to look. This same property of matching by
semantic similarity is what allows the patch features of \cref{subsec:dino} to put two
images into correspondence without any geometric prior, of which the egocentric to
exocentric setting is the most demanding instance.

\paragraph{Ego and ego-exo datasets.}
Large-scale egocentric video collections established first-person perception as a serious research programme.
EPIC-KITCHENS-100, assembled by Damen et al. (2021) \cite{damen2021epickitchens100}, provides over 100 hours of annotated kitchen activity with dense action labels and per-pixel hand-object masks, anchoring egocentric benchmarks for action recognition and object interaction.
Ego4D, published by Grauman et al. in 2022 \cite{grauman2022ego4d}, scaled this ambition to 3,670 hours of egocentric footage recorded across 74 worldwide locations, introducing standardised benchmarks covering episodic memory, social interaction, and hand-object contact, and framing the field around past, present, and future reasoning.
Ego-Exo4D, published by the same group in 2024 \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d}, extended the collection to synchronised multi-camera recordings in which a wearable egocentric camera and multiple fixed exocentric cameras capture the same skilled human activities simultaneously.
The dataset enables a qualitatively new class of tasks that require relating observations across the two perspectives, including the \textit{Ego-Exo4D Correspondences} benchmark, which defines the cross-view object mask transfer task used as the evaluation protocol throughout this work.

\paragraph{Ego-exo correspondence methods.}
\label{sec:egoexo-methods}
The \textit{Ego-Exo4D Correspondences} benchmark \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d} defines the task as predicting the mask of a designated object in either an ego or exo destination view, given its annotated mask in a source frame of the other view.
The annotation protocol combines automated mask proposals with human verification across a curated set of object categories spanning tools, containers, and food items, producing per-frame ground-truth masks for both \textit{Ego2Exo} and \textit{Exo2Ego} transfer directions.

The official baselines reported with the benchmark are XSegTx and XMem+XSegTx \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d}.
XSegTx adapts SegSwap \cite{segswap}, a co-segmentation model trained to locate shared objects across image pairs, by fine-tuning it on paired ego-exo frames; it operates on individual frame pairs with no temporal state, requiring a labelled ego-exo correspondence dataset to learn the cross-view co-segmentation head.
XMem+XSegTx wraps XSegTx inside a temporal memory loop built on XMem \cite{cheng2022xmem}; XSegTx initialises an object appearance embedding from the source mask, and XMem propagates that embedding across destination frames through cross-attention retrieval, but the appearance model is again learned from paired supervision and is not transferable to unseen scenarios without retraining.

ObjectRelator, proposed by Fu et al. in 2025 \cite{fu2025objectrelator}, fine-tunes PSALM \cite{psalm2024eccv} with two additional modules, namely a Multimodal Condition Fusion (MCFuse) block that concatenates visual mask features with language category descriptions to sharpen localisation, and a self-supervised cross-view object alignment loss (XObjAlign) that enforces consistency between ego and exo appearance embeddings during training.
Language enters as an auxiliary conditioning signal inside MCFuse, but the correspondence mechanism itself is the jointly trained visual embedding space; removing the paired ego-exo supervision used to train XObjAlign leaves the alignment undefined.

O-MaMa, proposed by Mur-Labadia et al. in 2025 \cite{o-mama} and winner of the 2025 Ego-Exo4D Correspondences Challenge, generates a pool of candidate masks in the destination view using FastSAM, then ranks them via a Mask-Context Encoder that pools dense DINOv2 \cite{oquab2023dinov2} patch features over each candidate region to produce a compact object descriptor of fixed dimension.
An Ego-Exo Cross-Attention module then fuses the source and destination descriptors so that each view's representation can attend to the other before the final match is selected.
The matching head is trained with a contrastive loss that pulls corresponding ego-exo pairs together in a shared latent space while pushing apart negatives drawn by a Hard Negative Adjacent Mining strategy, which samples temporally nearby frames as negatives precisely because their visual content is similar enough to constitute challenging confounders.
The method reaches competitive benchmark performance with 1\% of ObjectRelator's trainable parameters, indicating that a compact matching head over a strong pretrained feature space recovers most of the correspondence signal; nonetheless, the cross-view latent alignment is entirely learned from paired ego-exo supervision, so DINOv2 features are not used off the shelf but are recalibrated by the trained Mask-Context Encoder and cross-attention weights.

V\textsuperscript{2}-SAM, proposed by Pan et al. (2025) \cite{pan2025v2sam}, adapts SAM2 \cite{ravi2024sam2} for cross-view segmentation through two learned prompt-generation experts.
V\textsuperscript{2}-Anchor extracts DINOv3 \cite{dinov3} patch features from both views, computes dense cosine similarity over foreground regions, and applies stratified point sampling with a minimum pairwise distance constraint to produce a spatially diverse set of coordinate correspondences with high confidence; these are projected into SAM2's coordinate space and passed to its prompt encoder as geometric anchor prompts.
V\textsuperscript{2}-Visual instead operates on appearance, combining a Feature Mapping branch that learns cross-attention over mask-pooled query features to produce semantically consistent representations with a Structural Mapping branch that uses feature-wise linear modulation to reconstruct target mask structure from geometric priors, together yielding a prompt guided by appearance.
A Post-hoc Cyclic Consistency Selector arbitrates between the two experts at inference time without additional learned parameters; it back-projects each expert's prediction to the source view via V\textsuperscript{2}-Anchor and selects the expert whose back-projected points land closest to the original source mask, thereby preferring the prediction that is most geometrically self-consistent.
All three modules are trained on 233K ego-exo frame pairs from Ego-Exo4D together with supplementary data from HANDAL-X and DAVIS-2017, using a combination of visual contrastive, structural constraint, and mask prediction losses; the geometry and appearance alignment encoded in V\textsuperscript{2}-Anchor and V\textsuperscript{2}-Visual are entirely learned, not a property of the DINOv3 backbone alone.

By the time of writing, LM-EEC, proposed by Hu et al. (2025) \cite{hu2025lmeec}, achieves the strongest published results on the benchmark, reaching 54.98 IoU on Ego2Exo and 65.77 IoU on Exo2Ego.
The model adapts SAM2 by replacing its simple prompt addition with a Memory-View Mixture-of-Experts (MV-MoE) fusion module, which treats features conditioned on memory and features specific to each view as two complementary experts and fuses them through a dual-branch routing mechanism, namely a channel-wise branch that applies global average pooling followed by separate MLPs to generate importance weights per channel, and a spatial branch that uses parallel convolutional maps to assign spatially varying weights; the two refined expert features are then summed to condition the segmentation decoder.
Rather than a single unified memory bank, LM-EEC maintains separate ego and exo memory stores; when either bank exceeds a capacity threshold, a compression step identifies the most temporally redundant feature pair by minimum Euclidean distance and replaces it with the average of the two adjacent frames, reducing bank length by one while preserving discriminative content.
Despite the name suggesting language involvement, there is no language component in the architecture; the MV-MoE routing weights, the dual-memory compression policy, and the full SAM2 backbone are trained end-to-end on 756 ego-exo training videos from Ego-Exo4D, with memory compression applied only at inference.

\paragraph{Common limitations.} The shared structure across all five prior approaches is that correspondence is reduced to a learned visual matching problem; each method trains a dedicated component on paired ego-exo data, specifically the SegSwap co-segmentation head in XSegTx, the XMem appearance embedding, the XObjAlign consistency loss in ObjectRelator, the contrastive matching head in O-MaMa, the prompt experts in V\textsuperscript{2}-SAM, and the MoE fusion module in LM-EEC, and none of these components transfers to a novel camera configuration or scenario without retraining.
This design choice also forecloses interpretability; the matching decision is encoded in opaque attention weights, and there is no intermediate representation that a user or auditor can inspect to verify why a particular object was selected.

\paragraph{Our approach.} We take a different route; no component of the pipeline described in \cref{sec:pipeline} is trained for the task. Natural language serves as the semantic bridge between views, so that a mask description generated in the source view can locate the object in the destination view through a composition of foundation models used entirely off the shelf. The bridge is human-readable, so any erroneous prediction can be traced to the specific block whose output is at fault. This framing is the logical conclusion of the theoretical background developed in \cref{sec:segmentation,sec:language-grounding,sec:foundation-models}, and the premise from which \cref{sec:pipeline} proceeds.
