\section{Related Works in Cross-View Object Correspondence}
\label{sec:related-works}

Before combining the learnings from the previous sections into our pipeline, we review related methods for cross-view object correspondence. We begin with the datasets that made the problem measurable, then walk through two families of solutions. We conclude with the common limitations that all approaches share, highlighting the gap that motivates the language-first paradigm of our pipeline.

\paragraph{Datasets.} \label{par:datasets} Cross-view data emerged from research in first person video understanding, itself motivated by the recent onset of personal devices with augmented reality capabilities. EPIC-KITCHENS-100 \cite{damen2021epickitchens100}, released in 2021, provides over 100 hours of kitchen activity with dense action labels, narrations, and hand and object bounding box annotations, enabling egocentric action recognition and object interaction. Ego4D \cite{grauman2022ego4d} followed in 2022 with 3,670 hours of egocentric footage from 74 worldwide locations across scenarios from shopping to gardening to sports, adding standardized benchmarks for episodic memory, social interaction, and action forecasting, and establishing a diverse and robust foundation for the field.

The decisive update came from the same group in 2024 with Ego-Exo4D \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d}, which added more than 1,280 hours of synchronized recordings that pair a wearable egocentric camera with one or more exocentric cameras filming skilled actors at once. Its annotations offer object masks across categories and sizes, from tools and containers to sport equipment, instruments, health products, and food. The mask frequency per video is also high, at one mask per object every 30 frames. The dataset introduced further tasks including pose estimation, key-step recognition, and our ego-exo object correspondence. To date, several approaches already have been developed.

\subsection{Geometric Approaches}
\label{subsec:matching}
\paragraph{Early methods.} Transferring a mask from one camera perspective to another can be interpreted in several ways. In \cref{sec:segmentation}, we framed it as segmentation conditioned  on another view. Another form is asking which pixel in one image of a scene corresponds to which pixel in the other image of the same scene. The \textit{SIFT} descriptor of Lowe in 2004 \cite{lowe2004sift}, followed by \textit{SURF} \cite{bay2008surf} in 2008 and \textit{ORB} \cite{rublee2011orb} in 2011 fall in this category, They detect a sparse set of keypoints that encode the neighborhood of each pixel into a vector built to be invariant to scale and rotation. Two images are then matched by pairing keypoints whose descriptors are nearest in this space. These methods remain remarkably durable, but their view invariances are designed rather than learned, and they degrade sharply under the wide changes in viewpoint and illumination that separate an egocentric from an exocentric camera. Learning methods replaced design in two stages. SuperPoint \cite{detone2018superpoint} trains a single network to detect keypoints and emit descriptors jointly, and SuperGlue \cite{sarlin2020superglue} matches two such sets with a graph network based on attention that reasons about all candidate pairs at once rather than matching each keypoint in isolation. Subsequently, LoFTR \cite{sun2021loftr}  removed detection entirely, applying  self-attention and cross-attention between two images to produce dense matches directly, so that correspondences can be recovered even in the textureless regions where keypoint detectors find nothing to anchor to.

\paragraph{RoMa.} The latest advancement of this group is Robust Dense Feature Matching (RoMa), introduced by Edstedt et al.\ in 2024 \cite{roma}, which carries the geometric paradigm to its extreme. Rather than a list of matched points, RoMa predicts a dense \textit{warp field} $\warp$ together with a confidence for each coordinate,
\begin{equation}
  (\warp, c) = f_\theta(\img_a, \img_b),
  \qquad
  \warp(\vv{x}) \in \R^2,
  \quad
  c(\vv{x}) \in [0,1],
  \label{eq:warp}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta$ is the matcher applied to a source image $\img_a$ and a target image $\img_b$. For every coordinate $\vv{x}$ in the source, $\warp(\vv{x})$ returns the coordinate in the target it corresponds to. This makes $\warp$ a complete deformation carrying one image onto the other. The confidence $c(\vv{x})$ reports how
reliable that single match is, with values near one where the geometry is certain and near zero where the source point is occluded or has no counterpart. RoMa begins by estimating the field from image features, which give it a semantic anchor that survives wide changes in viewpoint, and then concludes with a refinement to pixel-level precision. Yet RoMa's performance on Ego-Exo4D is modest, as shown in \cref{fig:roma-examples} and reported by \cite{o-mama}. The viewpoint gap between an egocentric and an exocentric camera leaves too few confident correspondences for a dense matcher to anchor on, so the warp collapses precisely where it would be needed most. This shortfall is what steers our pipeline towards grounding the object through language rather than geometry (\cref{chap:pipeline}), and we leave dense matching as a complementary signal only for future investigation (\cref{chap:conclusions}).

\subsection{Trained Feature Fusion Architectures}
\label{subsec:feature-fusion}
Matching objects at the pixel value is inefficient because of the extreme viewpoint changes between egocentric and exocentric videos, which result in severe distortions in color, shape, and position. Therefore,
most methods that address the ego-exo4D correspondence challenge share a common paradigm; building an architecture that fuses source and destination features and training that fusion on the labeled data of Ego-Exo4D.

\paragraph{Official baselines.} The official Ego-Exo4D work presents two baselines: XSegTx and XView-XMem \cite{grauman2024egoexo4d}.
XSegTx adapts SegSwap \cite{segswap}, a co-segmentation model trained to locate shared objects across image pairs, by conditioning it on the object of interest, passing the query mask alongside the two frames as a third input and training the co-segmentation head on the labelled correspondence data. It operates on individual frame pairs with no temporal state. XView-XMem instead builds directly on XMem \cite{cheng2022xmem}, originally designed to track a target object across a video given its mask in the first frame. The baseline adapts XMem to propagate the object across the two views, supplying the ground-truth mask for one view at each frame and tracking the corresponding object in the other through its memory retrieval.

\paragraph{2025 challenge submissions.} ObjectRelator, \cite{fu2025objectrelator}, adapts PSALM \cite{psalm2024eccv} by training two additional modules. The first is a Multimodal Condition Fusion (MCFuse) block that fuses visual mask features with language category descriptions through cross-attention to sharpen localisation, and the second is the XObjAlign loss, designed to align objects across views through self-supervision and enforce consistency between egocentric and exocentric object embeddings during training. 

\newpage
\noindent Interestingly, language is used as an auxiliary conditioning signal inside MCFuse, but the correspondence mechanism itself is the jointly trained visual embedding space.

Object Mask Matching (O-MaMa), proposed by Mur-Labadia et al. in 2025 \cite{o-mama}, generates a pool of candidate masks in the destination view using FastSAM \cite{zhao2023fastsegment}, then ranks them through a Mask-Context Encoder that pools dense DINOv2 \cite{oquab2023dinov2} patch features over each candidate region to produce a compact object descriptor of fixed dimension. Its architecture is depicted in \cref{fig:related-works-architectures}. O-MaMa then uses a Cross-Attention module to fuse the source and destination descriptors so that each view's representation can attend to the other before the final match is selected. Finally, there is a matching head trained with a contrastive loss to pull corresponding pairs from the two views together in a shared latent space while pushing apart negatives. The negative examples are drawn by a Hard Negative Adjacent Mining strategy, which selects the masks spatially adjacent to the target in the destination view as negatives precisely because their visual content is similar enough to constitute challenging confounders. Notably, the method reached state of the art results and won the 2025 Ego-Exo4D Correspondences Challenge with 1\% of ObjectRelator's trainable parameters, suggesting that compact matching components over a strong pretrained feature space are enough to complete the correspondence. 

\paragraph{Recent progress.} In late 2025, V\textsuperscript{2}-SAM adapted SAM2 for cross-view segmentation through three experts, each conditioning a shared SAM2 mask decoder on a different prompt. V\textsuperscript{2}-Anchor matches DINOv3 patch features across views by dense cosine similarity and stratified point sampling, projecting the resulting high-confidence correspondences into SAM2's coordinate space as geometric anchor prompts for the Anchor Expert. V\textsuperscript{2}-Visual instead reconstructs the target mask structure from geometric priors through a Feature Mapping and a Structural Mapping branch, yielding an appearance-guided prompt for the Visual Expert. A third Fusion Expert combines both prompts, and at inference a Post-hoc Cyclic Consistency Selector arbitrates among the three.

\newpage
By spring 2026, LM-EEC of Hu et al. \cite{hu2025lmeec} achieves the state of the art on the ego-exo correspondence benchmark, reaching 54.98 IoU on Ego2Exo and 65.77 IoU on Exo2Ego on Ego-Exo4D official test splits. The model adapts SAM2 by replacing its simple prompt addition with a Memory-View Mixture-of-Experts (MV-MoE) fusion module. Its architecture is presented in \cref{fig:related-works-architectures}. The MV-MoE module treats features conditioned on memory and features specific to each view as two complementary experts. The first operates over channels, applies global average pooling and separate MLPs to generate importance weights per channel. The second works on the image using  parallel convolutional maps to assign spatially varying weights. The two features are then summed to condition the segmentation decoder. Moreover, rather than a single unified memory bank, LM-EEC maintains separate egocentric and exocentric memory stores and proposes a clever compression step that identifies the most temporally redundant feature pairs and replaces them with the average of the two adjacent frames. 

\subsection{Common Limitations}
\label{subsec:common-limitations}
Each method above trains a dedicated component on fusing visual features across the ego and exo views. The co-segmentation head in XSegTx and the cross-view propagation adapted in XView-XMem are learned from a labelled correspondence dataset, so neither transfers to an unseen scenario without retraining. ObjectRelator depends on the XObjAlign loss to define its alignment across views, which collapses once the paired supervision used to train it is removed. O-MaMa's reliance on off-the-shelf features is only apparent, since its cross-view alignment is entirely learned and the DINOv2 features are recalibrated by the trained Mask-Context Encoder and cross-attention weights. The geometry and appearance alignment encoded in V\textsuperscript{2}-SAM's V\textsuperscript{2}-Anchor and V\textsuperscript{2}-Visual prompt generators is likewise a property of training rather than of the DINOv3 backbone alone, and LM-EEC trains its MV-MoE routing, memory policy, and full SAM2 backbone jointly on the benchmark videos. None of these fusion components transfers to a novel camera configuration, for instance two exocentric cameras, or scenario without retraining. Additionally, a trained feature fusion design severely limits interpretability. The matching decision is encoded in attention weights, and there is no intermediate representation other than attention maps that a user can inspect to verify why a particular object was selected. 

\paragraph{Our approach.} We take a different route, effectively into a new paradigm. First, no component of our  pipeline is trained for the task. Second, natural language serves as the semantic bridge between views, another idea to date unexplored in the field. In the next chapters, we demonstrate that a mask description generated in the source view can reliably locate the object in the destination view through a composition of foundation models, using entirely their off the shelf inference capabilities. As a result, the bridge between views is readable by humans, and any incorrect mask prediction can be traced to the specific block whose output is erroneous. This makes our pipeline the first of its kind, and opens up a new paradigm for future research.
